# Pregnancy After Recurrent Loss: What Your History Tells Us About Your Care

Audience: Patients currently pregnant after two or more prior pregnancy losses

Format: OpenMFM Presentation Standard v1.0 patient presentation

Status: Approved and produced as `index.html`
Planned length: 17 slides

## Educational design

- Single most important message: The exact story of each prior pregnancy loss helps the care team choose the right evaluation, reassurance plan, and pregnancy-specific monitoring. A history of recurrent loss does not mean that every patient needs every test or treatment.
- Common misconceptions: The losses were the patient’s fault; all recurrent losses have the same cause; bed rest prevents miscarriage; aspirin, heparin, or progesterone help everyone; “high risk” always means frequent tests or early delivery.
- Prerequisite knowledge: None. Clinical terms will be defined on first use.
- Key decisions: What records are needed; which loss pattern is present; what evaluation has already been completed; whether APS, thyroid disease, diabetes, uterine anatomy, cervical history, or genetics changes care; how to create an early-pregnancy reassurance plan; what later surveillance is justified by the patient’s actual risk factors.
- Six-month memory: “My story is data. My care plan should match my pattern, not just my number of losses.”
- Visual language: A calm dark OpenMFM canvas, cyan “care pathway” line, warm rose used sparingly for urgent symptoms, and diagrams rather than dense bullet lists.

## Slide outline

### 1. Your history is the map for this pregnancy

- Objective: Establish the patient-centered promise of the presentation.
- Key teaching point: The timing, findings, and circumstances of each prior loss guide current care more than the count alone.
- Visual concept: A route map with three stops: listen, identify the pattern, build the plan.
- Anticipated references: ACOG Repeated Miscarriages FAQ; ASRM Committee Opinion 2026.

### 2. Recurrent pregnancy loss means two or more losses, but definitions differ

- Objective: Define the topic without making the patient police terminology.
- Key teaching point: ACOG and ASRM use two or more pregnancy losses; some international guidance uses three early miscarriages. Care should not be delayed because labels differ.
- Visual concept: Two overlapping definition cards converging on “ask for evaluation after two losses.”
- Anticipated references: ACOG FAQ100, reviewed May 2026; ASRM 2026; RCOG Green-top 17 (2023).

### 3. Most miscarriages are not caused by something you did

- Objective: Directly address guilt and self-blame.
- Key teaching point: Many early losses result from chance chromosome differences; work, ordinary exercise, sex, stress, or an argument do not cause miscarriage.
- Visual concept: “Not your fault” center statement surrounded by crossed-out myths.
- Anticipated references: ACOG Early Pregnancy Loss; ACOG Repeated Miscarriages.

### 4. One pregnancy timeline can reveal several different pathways

- Objective: Explain why gestational age matters.
- Key teaching point: A positive test only, an early ultrasound loss, painless cervical opening, PPROM, placental complications, and later fetal death are different clinical patterns.
- Visual concept: Horizontal pregnancy timeline with branching icons and plain-language definitions.
- Anticipated references: ASRM 2026; ESHRE RPL guideline 2023; source material.

### 5. For each loss, your care team needs the same eight facts

- Objective: Give patients a practical record-gathering checklist.
- Key teaching point: Date, gestational age, location, heartbeat, symptoms/pattern, management, pathology/genetics, and partner/conception details create a usable phenotype.
- Visual concept: Eight-segment “history wheel” plus QR/link to the interactive Visit History Builder.
- Anticipated references: ASRM 2026; ACOG Repeated Miscarriages; source material.

### 6. Bring the records that can change the plan

- Objective: Reduce duplicate testing and uncertainty.
- Key teaching point: Ultrasound reports, operative notes, pregnancy-tissue chromosome results, placental pathology, APS labs with dates, uterine imaging, and prior consultation notes often matter more than memory alone.
- Visual concept: A patient folder that lights up the decision nodes it informs.
- Anticipated references: ASRM 2026; ESHRE 2023.

### 7. The evaluation is targeted, not an “everything panel”

- Objective: Distinguish useful evaluation from low-value testing.
- Key teaching point: Uterine-cavity assessment and selected genetic, APS, thyroid, and diabetes evaluation may be appropriate; broad inherited-thrombophilia, immune, microbiome, or other add-on panels are not routine RPL testing.
- Visual concept: Two lanes, “can change care” and “usually does not help,” with clinician-review labels rather than self-order instructions.
- Anticipated references: ASRM Committee Opinion 2026; ESHRE 2023.

### 8. APS is important because a confirmed diagnosis changes treatment

- Objective: Explain antiphospholipid syndrome safely.
- Key teaching point: APS requires the right clinical history plus persistent laboratory findings. Confirmed obstetric APS may be treated with low-dose aspirin and heparin under specialist care; empiric anticoagulation does not improve unexplained RPL.
- Visual concept: A two-key lock: clinical criteria + repeat antibodies, opening a treatment pathway.
- Anticipated references: ASRM 2026; ACOG Repeated Miscarriages.

### 9. Thyroid disease and diabetes matter when they are present and uncontrolled

- Objective: Frame endocrine care without overtesting.
- Key teaching point: Overt thyroid disease and uncontrolled diabetes should be treated; thyroid or glucose testing is selected based on history, symptoms, prior tissue results, and risk factors.
- Visual concept: Two simple gauges moving into pregnancy-safe target zones.
- Anticipated references: ASRM 2026; ACOG Repeated Miscarriages.

### 10. Painless second-trimester loss calls for a cervical-history review

- Objective: Help patients recognize a pattern worth flagging.
- Key teaching point: Painless dilation, early membrane rupture, or certain cervical procedures may lead to cervical-length surveillance or a cerclage discussion. The exact pathway depends on the prior obstetric history and current findings.
- Visual concept: Cervix timeline from history review to 12–14-week or 16–24-week decision points, labeled “individualized.”
- Anticipated references: ACOG cervical insufficiency guidance; SMFM Consult Series #70 (2024); source material.

### 11. Early care should confirm location, dating, and viability

- Objective: Set expectations for the first weeks.
- Key teaching point: Early transvaginal ultrasound is used to confirm an intrauterine pregnancy and establish dating/viability; repeat timing is individualized so scans are informative rather than prematurely alarming.
- Visual concept: Three ultrasound milestones with a soft reassurance band below.
- Anticipated references: ACOG Early Pregnancy Loss; ESHRE 2023.

### 12. Support is a component of care, not an extra

- Objective: Normalize anxiety and grief in a new pregnancy.
- Key teaching point: A clear contact plan, agreed scan schedule, mental-health screening, and grief-informed communication belong in prenatal care after loss.
- Visual concept: A support “care circle” connecting patient, partner/support person, OB, MFM, and mental-health resources.
- Anticipated references: ACOG Repeated Miscarriages; ACOG Anxiety and Pregnancy; ESHRE 2023.

### 13. Progesterone helps a specific group, not everyone

- Objective: Prevent overgeneralization of progesterone evidence.
- Key teaching point: Routine progesterone has not shown benefit for every patient with unexplained RPL. Vaginal micronized progesterone may be offered or considered when early-pregnancy bleeding occurs in a patient with prior miscarriage, after ultrasound and clinician review.
- Visual concept: A “right patient + right moment” intersection.
- Anticipated references: ASRM 2026; NICE NG126, updated June 2026; RCOG 2023.

### 14. Aspirin and heparin are not universal miscarriage treatments

- Objective: Separate RPL treatment from preeclampsia prevention.
- Key teaching point: Heparin plus aspirin is used for confirmed APS. Low-dose aspirin may also be recommended for preeclampsia prevention when standard risk factors are present, but RPL alone is not a reason to self-start either medicine.
- Visual concept: Two medication pathways with distinct indications and a “do not self-start” bridge.
- Anticipated references: ASRM 2026; ACOG Low-Dose Aspirin Use During Pregnancy.

### 15. Later pregnancy monitoring follows the risk factors you actually have

- Objective: Set realistic expectations for second- and third-trimester care.
- Key teaching point: Anatomy ultrasound and routine prenatal care remain the base. Growth scans, cervical surveillance, Doppler studies, NST/BPP testing, or earlier delivery are added for APS, hypertension, diabetes, prior placental disease, cervical risk, fetal growth problems, or other current indications. Unexplained RPL alone does not automatically require every test or early delivery.
- Visual concept: A base-care platform with modular surveillance blocks added only when triggered.
- Anticipated references: ACOG outpatient antenatal fetal surveillance guidance; SMFM Consult #70; ASRM 2026.

### 16. Questions to ask your MFM specialist

- Objective: Turn the presentation into a visit agenda.
- Key teaching point: Patients should leave with a named pattern, a records/testing plan, an early reassurance plan, medication indications, warning signs, later monitoring criteria, and a communication plan.
- Visual concept: Seven large question cards with checkboxes.
- Anticipated references: Synthesis of cited guidance.

### 17. Your plan should be specific, evidence-based, and kind

- Objective: Close with hope and boundaries.
- Key teaching point: More than half of RPL may remain unexplained, yet future live birth is still common. Good care combines evidence-based evaluation with support and avoids unproven treatment.
- Visual concept: “Your story → your plan” pathway ending in a calm horizon, followed by expandable references, educational disclaimer, and privacy statement.
- Anticipated references: ACOG Repeated Miscarriages; ASRM 2026; ESHRE 2023.

## Evidence corrections applied to the supplied notes

1. Recurrent loss alone is not presented as an automatic indication for low-dose aspirin. Aspirin is tied to confirmed APS treatment or established preeclampsia-risk criteria.
2. Uncomplicated unexplained RPL is not presented as an automatic indication for serial growth scans, antenatal testing at 36 weeks, or delivery at 39 weeks. Later surveillance is tied to the patient’s actual comorbidities, prior phenotype, and current pregnancy findings.
3. Progesterone is not presented as routine treatment for asymptomatic unexplained RPL. The supported scenario is prior miscarriage plus bleeding in the current early pregnancy, with shared decision-making and confirmation of an intrauterine pregnancy where required by the chosen guideline.
4. Routine inherited-thrombophilia testing and empiric anticoagulation are excluded from the standard RPL pathway.
5. The 2026 ASRM committee opinion supersedes the older 2012 ASRM document for evaluation and treatment framing.
