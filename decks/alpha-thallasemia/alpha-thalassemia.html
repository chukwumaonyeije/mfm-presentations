Alpha thalassemia trait in pregnancy

Most people with this trait feel well and have healthy pregnancies. A carrier result raises two separate questions.

How is my health?

Your blood count and iron levels help explain small red blood cells or mild anemia.

What could my baby inherit?

Your partner’s gene pattern helps your care team estimate the chance of a more serious condition.

Sources GeneReviews · TIF guidelines

Understanding your result

With trait, two of four gene copies work

Hemoglobin is the protein in red blood cells that carries oxygen. Alpha-globin genes give the body instructions for making part of that protein.

Why you may feel well

Two working copies usually make enough alpha globin for daily life. The trait is common worldwide and is often found on routine blood work.

What may show up

Your red cells may be smaller than average (microcytosis). Your hemoglobin level may also be mildly low (anemia).

Sources GeneReviews

Understanding your result

Small red cells alone cannot tell you whether you need iron

Iron deficiency and alpha thalassemia trait can both cause small red cells. You can have both at the same time.

What guides treatment?

Keep taking prenatal vitamins as prescribed. Ask about your iron results before starting extra iron for anemia.

What confirms the trait?

Routine hemoglobin testing can look normal. A DNA test can identify changes in the alpha-globin genes.

Sources GeneReviews · TIF guidelines

Inheritance

The same two changes can sit in different places

You have two copies of chromosome 16, one from each genetic parent. Each chromosome usually carries two alpha-globin genes.

Together on one chromosome (cis)

A parent with this pattern can pass on a chromosome with two working copies or a chromosome with both copies inactive.

Split across chromosomes (trans)

In this common deletion pattern, either chromosome a parent passes on carries one working copy.

Sources GeneReviews · TIF guidelines

Inheritance

Two cis carriers have a 1 in 4 chance of the major form in each pregnancy

This example applies when both genetic parents carry two inactive copies together. Each square is equally likely.

Read the grid

The top row shows the chromosome one parent passes on. The left column shows the chromosome from the other parent.

Other pairings give different chances

Three inactive copies usually cause hemoglobin H (HbH) disease. If the other parent has four working copies, the baby can inherit a carrier pattern but will have at least two working copies.

Your care plan

Your family’s results guide the next test

Ask whether the baby’s other genetic parent needs alpha-globin DNA testing. The exact changes help your team interpret the result.

If you want a fetal diagnosis

Chorionic villus sampling (CVS) tests a small sample of placenta. Amniocentesis tests fluid around the baby. The sample can be checked for the family’s gene changes.

You decide with your care team

Ask how timing, procedure risks and the information you would gain fit your goals.

Sources GeneReviews · TIF guidelines · Perinatal consensus

Targeted monitoring

Ultrasound checks for signs of fetal anemia

In the major form, also called hemoglobin Bart’s syndrome, the baby makes too little functional hemoglobin. Severe anemia reduces oxygen delivery.

Blood flow in the brain

Doppler ultrasound measures blood speed in the middle cerebral artery (MCA). Faster flow can signal anemia.

Fluid buildup (hydrops)

The team looks for fluid under the skin, around the lungs or heart, and in the belly. Ask what each scan will assess and when to return.

Sources TIF guidelines · Perinatal consensus

If the major form is confirmed

A fetal therapy team can explain your choices

This conversation is for families facing a confirmed severe diagnosis. A fetal medicine specialist and a pediatric blood specialist can meet with you together.

What to weigh

Untreated hydrops can threaten both your health and the baby’s life. Fetal transfusions can allow survival. Outcomes vary, and surviving children generally need regular transfusions and treatment for iron buildup.

What matters to you?

Ask about expected outcomes, access to treatment and support for your family.

Sources TIF guidelines · Perinatal consensus

Everyday care

Bring your results and know when to seek care

Share any family history of anemia or thalassemia. Keep your lab and ultrasound appointments so your team can adjust the plan as results arrive.

At routine prenatal visits

Your team follows your anemia and blood pressure. If gestational diabetes develops, they will treat it and monitor the baby’s growth as indicated.

Seek medical care immediately

Chest pain, trouble breathing, fainting, or sudden overwhelming tiredness can be urgent in pregnancy.
