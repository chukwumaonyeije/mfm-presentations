# A Mass Near Your Baby's Diaphragm

## OpenMFM Presentation Standard v1.0 — Outline for Approval

**Working title:** A Mass Near Your Baby's Diaphragm: Intra-abdominal Pulmonary Sequestration or CPAM?

**Subtitle:** A patient guide to the ultrasound findings, the diagnostic process, and what usually happens next

**Audience:** Pregnant patients and families counseled about a fetal upper-abdominal or lower-chest mass with a differential diagnosis of intra-abdominal extralobar pulmonary sequestration (IEPS) versus congenital pulmonary airway malformation (CPAM, formerly CCAM)

**Format planned after approval:** A self-contained, responsive HTML presentation with keyboard and touch navigation, URL hash navigation, progress bar, slide counter, print/PDF mode, accessible HTML/CSS/SVG diagrams, reduced-motion support, a references panel, and patient-appropriate language.

## Stage 0 — Educational Design

### Single most important message

These two uncommon developmental lung lesions can look alike before birth. The most useful clue is where the mass sits and where its feeding artery comes from, but sometimes the final name remains uncertain until imaging or evaluation after birth. Most babies without hydrops or severe compression do well with planned follow-up.

### What the learner should remember six months later

The scan is not looking for a label alone. It is answering four practical questions: **Where is the mass? What supplies it with blood? Is it affecting the baby now? What care will be needed after birth?**

### Common misconceptions to correct

1. A prenatal mass automatically means cancer.
2. “Pulmonary” means the mass must be inside the chest.
3. Ultrasound texture alone can always distinguish sequestration from CPAM.
4. Fetal MRI is always required or always provides a definitive diagnosis.
5. A CVR above 1.6 automatically means fetal treatment is needed.
6. A lesion that becomes difficult to see later in pregnancy has necessarily disappeared.
7. Every baby needs emergency surgery immediately after delivery.
8. Diagnostic uncertainty means the care team has no plan.

### Prerequisite knowledge

No medical background is assumed. The presentation will define the diaphragm, Doppler ultrasound, systemic feeding artery, CPAM, pulmonary sequestration, hydrops, fetal MRI, and postnatal imaging in plain language.

### Key clinical decisions

1. Does the mass arise above or below the diaphragm?
2. Can Doppler show a feeding artery from the aorta or one of its branches?
3. Is the lesion growing, shifting nearby organs, compressing normal lung, or associated with fluid buildup or hydrops?
4. Would fetal MRI materially improve localization, lung-volume assessment, or delivery planning?
5. Is routine MFM surveillance enough, or is fetal therapy consultation needed?
6. Where should delivery occur?
7. What imaging and pediatric surgical review should occur after birth?

### Concepts that require diagrams

- Chest, diaphragm, and upper-abdominal anatomy
- Side-by-side IEPS and CPAM anatomy
- Aortic versus pulmonary blood-supply pathways
- Ultrasound/Doppler diagnostic pathway
- Pregnancy-to-newborn care timeline

## Evidence Audit of the Supplied Material

| Supplied concept | Assessment | Revision for the patient presentation |
|---|---|---|
| “CCAM” is the diagnosis being compared with IEPS | Terminology needs updating | Use **CPAM** as the current term and explain once that it was formerly called CCAM. |
| The feeding vessel definitively separates IEPS from CPAM | Directionally useful but too absolute | A systemic artery from the aorta strongly supports sequestration. CPAMs usually have pulmonary blood supply, but hybrid lesions can contain CPAM tissue and systemic arterial supply. The diagnosis may remain provisional before birth. |
| Ultrasound with color or power Doppler is the initial diagnostic test | Consistent | Retain. Emphasize careful localization relative to the diaphragm and tracing the vessel in multiple planes. |
| Fetal MRI is the definitive next step when ultrasound is indeterminate | Too absolute | MRI is a useful complementary study for anatomy, lesion extent, and remaining lung volume. It may not always resolve the diagnosis or show a very small feeding vessel. Use when it will answer a management question. |
| IEPS can mimic microcystic CPAM and hybrid lesions occur | Consistent | Retain in plain language: some lesions share features, and final diagnosis may require postnatal imaging or pathology. Do not foreground an imprecise hybrid-lesion percentage. |
| CVR >1.6 predicts hydrops and respiratory distress | Needs qualification | Explain that CVR is a risk-estimation tool developed for fetal echogenic lung lesions, particularly intrathoracic CPAM. A value above 1.6 raises concern for hydrops but does not mandate intervention; serial findings and the whole fetal examination guide care. |
| IEPS can cause massive pleural effusions requiring shunting | Potentially conflates lesion locations | Pleural effusion and fetal intervention are better-established concerns for **intrathoracic** bronchopulmonary sequestration. A small isolated intra-abdominal sequestration usually behaves differently. Present intervention only as a rare, anatomy-dependent scenario. |
| Postnatal surgery is often pursued because of infection and malignancy risk | Requires balanced counseling | State that every baby needs postnatal confirmation and pediatric review. Observation versus elective resection is individualized by diagnosis, symptoms, anatomy, feeding vessel, center practice, and family discussion. Avoid presenting malignancy as an expected outcome. |
| A normal prenatal ultrasound rules out neither lesion | True but low-value for this audience | Omit from the main narrative because the audience already has a detected lesion; mention only in the evidence panel if needed. |

## Slide-by-Slide Outline

### Slide 1 — The finding is real; the final name may still be uncertain

- **Objective:** Open with the exact patient experience and reduce avoidable anxiety.
- **Key teaching point:** An echogenic mass near the diaphragm can represent more than one developmental condition. The immediate task is to define its anatomy and determine whether it is affecting the baby.
- **Visual concept:** Calm fetal silhouette with a highlighted region at the lower chest/upper abdomen and a four-question pathway: location → blood supply → effect → plan.
- **Anticipated references:** ISUOG Patient Information: Cystic Lung Lesions; Xu et al. 2019.

### Slide 2 — These are developmental lung lesions, not a prenatal cancer diagnosis

- **Objective:** Define the two possibilities in reassuring, accurate language.
- **Key teaching point:** IEPS is a separate piece of lung-like tissue with no normal airway connection and its own systemic artery. CPAM is an area of abnormally developed airways within the lung.
- **Visual concept:** Two simple tissue diagrams labeled “separate lung-like tissue” and “abnormal airways within lung.”
- **Anticipated references:** ISUOG Patient Information; fetal thoracic imaging reviews.

### Slide 3 — CPAM is the current name for what was called CCAM

- **Objective:** Prevent terminology from becoming a source of confusion across reports and internet searches.
- **Key teaching point:** CPAM and CCAM refer to the same family of lesions; CPAM is the preferred modern term.
- **Visual concept:** Name-change card: CCAM → CPAM, with one plain-language definition.
- **Anticipated references:** Contemporary congenital lung-malformation literature.

### Slide 4 — The diaphragm is the first landmark

- **Objective:** Teach why lesion location matters.
- **Key teaching point:** The diaphragm separates the chest from the abdomen. IEPS can sit below it, often near the adrenal gland, while CPAM usually develops within a lung above it.
- **Visual concept:** Original SVG cross-section of fetal chest, diaphragm, lungs, liver, stomach, kidneys, and adrenal regions.
- **Anticipated references:** Xu et al. 2019; fetal MRI review literature.

### Slide 5 — The feeding artery is the strongest prenatal clue

- **Objective:** Explain the key diagnostic discriminator without overpromising certainty.
- **Key teaching point:** Doppler can trace blood flow. A vessel arising from the aorta or an aortic branch strongly favors sequestration; CPAM usually receives pulmonary circulation.
- **Visual concept:** Animated-on-screen/static-in-print vessel map: heart → pulmonary artery → CPAM versus aorta → systemic feeding artery → IEPS.
- **Anticipated references:** Xu et al. 2019; congenital lung-malformation imaging reviews.

### Slide 6 — Why can the two diagnoses look alike?

- **Objective:** Normalize diagnostic uncertainty.
- **Key teaching point:** Both may appear as a bright, solid-looking mass when cysts are too small to resolve. Some lesions also share features of both conditions.
- **Visual concept:** Three-panel texture diagram: microcystic CPAM, solid-appearing IEPS, and hybrid overlap.
- **Anticipated references:** Kane et al. 2020; Xu et al. 2019; pathologic series of hybrid lesions.

### Slide 7 — Ultrasound answers more than “What is it?”

- **Objective:** Show what the MFM examination is actually evaluating.
- **Key teaching point:** The scan documents location, size, cysts, feeding vessel, diaphragm, nearby organs, normal lung, heart position, amniotic fluid, and signs of fetal stress.
- **Visual concept:** Radial ultrasound checklist around a fetal torso diagram, with a small “Doppler” callout.
- **Anticipated references:** ISUOG patient information; standard fetal lung-lesion assessment literature.

### Slide 8 — MRI is useful when it will answer a remaining question

- **Objective:** Set realistic expectations for fetal MRI.
- **Key teaching point:** MRI can clarify the mass’s relationship to the diaphragm and organs and estimate normal lung volume. It complements ultrasound; it is not automatically required and may not provide a final diagnosis.
- **Visual concept:** Ultrasound and MRI as two overlapping lenses, each with strengths and limits.
- **Anticipated references:** Gerall et al. 2022; fetal thoracic MRI reviews.

### Slide 9 — The most important question is whether the baby is being affected

- **Objective:** Reframe surveillance around physiology rather than the label.
- **Key teaching point:** Most concern comes from growth of the mass, compression, heart shift, fluid buildup, hydrops, or reduced normal lung volume, not from the name alone.
- **Visual concept:** Green/amber/red monitoring gauge with normal anatomy, mass effect, and fetal compromise.
- **Anticipated references:** Zhu et al. 2022; Kane et al. 2020; BPS natural-history series.

### Slide 10 — CVR estimates risk; it does not decide treatment by itself

- **Objective:** Explain a number patients may see in the ultrasound report.
- **Key teaching point:** CVR compares lesion volume with the baby’s head circumference. A CVR above 1.6 has been associated with a higher risk of hydrops in fetal lung lesions, but location, cyst pattern, serial change, and the entire fetal examination matter.
- **Visual concept:** Step-by-step, non-calculator diagram of lesion measurements → estimated volume → head circumference → trend over time.
- **Anticipated references:** Crombleholme et al. 2002; Kane et al. 2020; Zhu et al. 2022.

### Slide 11 — Follow-up watches the trend, not a single snapshot

- **Objective:** Prepare patients for serial MFM visits.
- **Key teaching point:** Repeat ultrasound tracks lesion size, feeding vessel, fluid, fetal growth, heart position/function, and hydrops. The visit schedule is individualized because these lesions can grow, plateau, or become less conspicuous.
- **Visual concept:** Gestational timeline with repeat scans and decision gates rather than a rigid universal schedule.
- **Anticipated references:** ISUOG patient information; congenital lung-malformation surveillance studies.

### Slide 12 — Fetal treatment is uncommon and depends on the complication

- **Objective:** Discuss severe scenarios without implying they are expected.
- **Key teaching point:** Most stable, nonhydropic fetuses are monitored. When a large chest lesion causes hydrops or a dominant cyst/fluid collection creates severe compression, a fetal therapy center may discuss steroids, drainage, shunting, or selected vascular treatment according to lesion type and anatomy.
- **Visual concept:** Escalation ladder: surveillance → fetal center review → complication-specific therapy.
- **Anticipated references:** Fetal therapy series; BPS intervention literature; CPAM management reviews.

### Slide 13 — Delivery planning is based on the baby's expected needs

- **Objective:** Explain how prenatal findings shape place and timing of birth.
- **Key teaching point:** A small stable lesion often does not change the route or timing of delivery. Significant mass effect, hydrops, or concern for breathing after birth may favor delivery at a tertiary center with neonatology and pediatric surgery.
- **Visual concept:** Two-path delivery map: routine obstetric plan versus tertiary-center plan.
- **Anticipated references:** ISUOG patient information; congenital lung-malformation outcome studies.

### Slide 14 — The diagnosis is confirmed after birth

- **Objective:** Set expectations for newborn evaluation.
- **Key teaching point:** The newborn team examines breathing and feeding. Ultrasound, chest radiography, CT angiography, or MRI may be used later to define the lesion and its vessels, even when prenatal imaging suggested a diagnosis.
- **Visual concept:** Birth-to-follow-up timeline: newborn exam → confirmatory imaging → pediatric surgery consultation.
- **Anticipated references:** Pediatric radiology and pediatric surgery reviews.

### Slide 15 — Surgery is a planned decision, not always an emergency

- **Objective:** Provide balanced counseling about postnatal treatment.
- **Key teaching point:** Symptomatic babies need prompt treatment. For a stable baby, the team discusses observation versus elective removal based on symptoms, anatomy, feeding vessel, diagnostic confidence, local practice, and family preferences.
- **Visual concept:** Shared-decision balance with “symptoms/anatomy” and “benefits/burdens of treatment.”
- **Anticipated references:** BPS/CPAM postnatal management reviews and cohort studies.

### Slide 16 — Most isolated, stable lesions have a favorable outlook

- **Objective:** Offer evidence-aligned reassurance.
- **Key teaching point:** Prognosis is usually favorable when there is no hydrops, severe compression, or major associated anomaly. The care plan remains important because prenatal appearance does not fully predict newborn symptoms or the exact postnatal diagnosis.
- **Visual concept:** Outcome drivers diagram with favorable factors and factors requiring closer planning.
- **Anticipated references:** Xu et al. 2019; congenital lung-malformation outcome studies.

### Slide 17 — Questions to ask your MFM specialist

- **Objective:** Give patients a practical counseling tool.
- **Key teaching point:** Patients should leave knowing the lesion’s location, size trend, blood supply, effect on the baby, role of MRI, follow-up schedule, delivery setting, and newborn plan.
- **Visual concept:** Eight large question cards suitable for saving or printing.
- **Planned questions:**
  1. Is the mass above or below the diaphragm?
  2. Can you see where its feeding artery comes from?
  3. Is it changing the heart position, normal lung volume, or nearby organs?
  4. Is there any fluid buildup or hydrops?
  5. Are you measuring a CVR, and what does the trend mean for this lesion?
  6. Would fetal MRI change the plan?
  7. Where should I deliver, and does this finding change timing or route of delivery?
  8. What imaging and specialists will my baby need after birth?
- **Anticipated references:** OpenMFM patient-counseling standard.

### Slide 18 — What to remember

- **Objective:** Reinforce the core counseling points.
- **Key teaching point:** Six takeaways: CPAM is the current term; location matters; systemic blood supply favors sequestration; overlap is real; fetal well-being guides prenatal care; postnatal confirmation completes the diagnosis.
- **Visual concept:** Six high-contrast takeaway cards.
- **Anticipated references:** Synthesis of core references.

### Slide 19 — Evidence, uncertainty, and educational disclaimer

- **Objective:** Separate established findings from areas of uncertainty and close safely.
- **Key teaching point:** Doppler identification of systemic arterial supply is a strong diagnostic clue, and serial surveillance for mass effect/hydrops is established practice. Uncertainty remains around prenatal classification of hybrid lesions, universal CVR thresholds, optimal surveillance intervals, and elective surgery for asymptomatic infants.
- **Visual concept:** Known / uncertain / individualized three-column panel followed by a short educational disclaimer and privacy statement.
- **Anticipated references:** Xu et al. 2019; Kane et al. 2020; Zhu et al. 2022; postnatal management reviews.

### Slide 20 — References

- **Objective:** Provide complete, updateable citations and direct links.
- **Key teaching point:** The final deck will distinguish patient-information sources, imaging evidence, prognostic evidence, and management literature.
- **Visual concept:** Categorized reference list with a persistent references-panel trigger in screen mode.
- **Anticipated references:** Complete list below.
