Start with normal blood flow

The heart sends blood to the lungs and the body

The heart has four chambers. The two lower chambers, called ventricles, pump blood out.

Understanding the diagnosis

TOF changes the wall and the pathway to the lungs

Tetralogy of Fallot (TOF) is a heart condition that develops before birth.

The fourth feature is thickening of the right lower chamber’s muscle. This usually develops later and is not needed to recognize TOF before birth.

Before and after birth

The placenta supplies oxygen before birth

During pregnancy, oxygen reaches your baby through the placenta and umbilical cord.

During pregnancy

The lungs do not yet do the work of breathing. Many babies with TOF remain stable while the team follows their heart.

After birth

The lungs take over. A temporary blood vessel, the ductus arteriosus, begins to close. Some babies need help getting enough blood to their lungs.

A reassuring scan during pregnancy does not tell us exactly how your baby will do after birth.

When there is another finding

An additional heart finding can change the plan

TOF does not always come with the same set of other heart differences.

TOF on its own

The ventricular hole and narrowed lung pathway are central findings. A shared valve is not part of every TOF diagnosis.

TOF with an AVSD

An atrioventricular septal defect (AVSD) affects the center of the heart. A complete AVSD includes holes and a shared valve instead of two separate valves.

If an AVSD is also present, the team checks valve leakage and discusses its genetic associations, including Down syndrome. Do not assume these findings apply to your baby.

The next scan

A detailed heart ultrasound guides care

A fetal echocardiogram, or “fetal echo,” is a special ultrasound of your baby’s heart.

  1. 01Map the heart

    Look carefully at chambers, valves, and blood vessels.

  2. 02Check the flow

    See how blood moves toward the lungs and body.

  3. 03Make a plan

    Discuss what is known and what still needs follow-up.

Your pregnancy specialist and a pediatric heart specialist work together. Some details become clearer later in pregnancy or after birth.

Genetics and your choices

Genetic testing is a choice you can discuss

Some babies with TOF have a chromosome or gene change. Many do not have an identified cause.

Screening estimates a chance

A blood screening test cannot rule out all genetic conditions linked with a heart difference.

Diagnostic testing looks more closely

Amniocentesis tests cells from the fluid around the baby. Chromosomal microarray looks for missing or extra pieces of DNA, including 22q11.2 deletion.

A genetic counselor can explain benefits, procedure risks, uncertain results, and what a normal result cannot exclude. You can accept or decline testing.

Following the pregnancy

Repeat scans look for changes that matter

Your team will set a schedule based on your baby’s heart and the rest of your pregnancy.

Heart follow-up

Check the pathway to the lungs, blood flow, and how well the heart is working.

Pregnancy follow-up

Follow growth and other findings. Tell your team about new concerns and follow their advice on fetal movement.

Your birth plan

Your baby’s needs determine where you give birth

The right hospital is one that can provide the care your baby is expected to need.

Agree on the hospital

Ask who will assess your baby, when the heart ultrasound will happen, and how a transfer would work if needed.

Agree on timing and birth method

TOF alone does not automatically mean an early birth or cesarean. Your obstetric and heart teams will advise you.

If urgent heart or breathing support is expected, plan birth where the required specialists and treatments are available.

The first hours

The first heart check after birth sets the next step

The team checks breathing, oxygen levels, and the heart’s anatomy.

  1. 01Examine

    Assess your baby and measure oxygen levels.

  2. 02Confirm

    Perform a newborn heart ultrasound.

  3. 03Support

    Provide monitoring, medicine, or an early procedure if needed.

What is prostaglandin?

Prostaglandin E1 is medicine that can keep the temporary ductus vessel open. Some babies need this to maintain blood flow to their lungs while the team plans treatment. It is not needed by every baby with TOF.

Planning treatment

Surgery is tailored to your baby’s heart

The aim is to close the ventricular hole and improve the route to the lungs.

A repair in infancy

Some babies can grow before their planned repair. The surgeon explains the approach and timing for your child.

Earlier or staged treatment

Other babies need support or a procedure soon after birth, followed by a later repair. A very narrow or closed lung pathway may change the plan.

Ask the team for outcomes that match your baby’s anatomy and other health findings. No single success rate describes every child.

Beyond the first repair

Heart care continues as your child grows

Many people with repaired TOF reach adulthood. Follow-up remains part of their care.

Keep heart appointments

The team follows heart rhythm, valve function, and exercise needs. Some people need further procedures.

Support the whole child

Discuss feeding, growth, development, and family support. Ask which resources are available near you.

Your next steps

Leave each visit with a clear next step

Use this checklist in your conversation with your team.

Before the next visit

  • Know the date and purpose of the next scan.
  • Write down your testing questions.
  • Ask whom to call between visits.

Before birth

  • Confirm the hospital and newborn heart plan.
  • Ask about feeding and family support.
  • Follow your maternity team’s instructions for labor symptoms or reduced fetal movement.

Questions to ask your MFM specialist

Ask how today’s findings change your baby’s plan

Bring the questions that matter most to your family.

About the diagnosis

  • How narrow is the pathway to the lungs?
  • Are there any other heart or body findings?
  • What could testing tell us, and what could it miss?

About the plan

  • Where should I give birth, and why?
  • Could my baby need treatment right away?
  • When can we meet the heart team and discuss feeding and support?

Your family • your care team

Your own team will personalize this information

A clear plan can make the next step feel more manageable.

You do not have to navigate this alone. Ask your team to explain the diagnosis and plan in words that make sense to you.

Educational information

This guide supports discussion with your clinicians. It cannot diagnose your baby or predict an individual outcome.
