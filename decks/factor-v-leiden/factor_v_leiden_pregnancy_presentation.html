How clotting works

Factor V Leiden slows one of the brakes on clotting

Factor V (say “factor five”) is a protein that helps stop bleeding. Leiden is the name of an inherited change in the gene for that protein.

When a vessel is injured

Factor V helps blood form a clot that seals the injury.

As the clot forms

Another protein helps switch factor V off. The Leiden form stays active longer, making a clot in a vein more likely.

How common is it?

About 1 in 20 people of European ancestry carry the change. How common it is varies by ancestry. Many carriers never have a clot.

Warning signs

Know when to call your care team and when to call 911

A clot deep in a leg vein is called deep vein thrombosis (DVT). Part of it can travel to the lungs and cause a pulmonary embolism (PE). Either can happen during pregnancy or after birth.

Get urgent assessment today

If one leg becomes swollen, painful, tender, warm or discolored, call your maternity team right away. If you cannot reach them promptly, seek urgent medical assessment.

Call 911 now

Sudden trouble breathing, chest pain (especially with a deep breath), coughing up blood or collapse can signal a lung clot. Your heart may race. Call emergency services immediately.

Your risk

Your clot history helps explain what your test means

Pregnancy itself makes blood clot more easily. Your daily risk is highest in the first weeks after birth. Bring your lab report and clot history to your visit.

Read your report

Heterozygous means one changed copy. Homozygous means two. Two copies carry a higher clot risk.

Tell the story of any past clot

Did it happen during pregnancy, while taking estrogen, after surgery or without a clear trigger? Tell your team about clots in close relatives, too.

Report changes

A hospital stay, surgery or a long spell of limited movement can change your prevention plan.

Preventing clots

Some people need heparin during pregnancy

Two people with the same gene result can need different care. These examples show what your team considers.

One copy, no previous clot

If you have no other major risks, your team may recommend watching for symptoms and reviewing your risk at visits.

A clot during a previous pregnancy

Your team will usually recommend preventive blood thinner injections during pregnancy and after birth. Two changed copies can also lead to a recommendation for prevention.

If heparin is prescribed

Low-molecular-weight heparin goes under the skin and does not cross the placenta. A nurse can teach you how and when to inject it and what bleeding to report. A new clot needs prompt treatment at a different dose.

Daily life

Build movement into your usual day

Most people can keep working and stay active during pregnancy. Ask about activities that fit your pregnancy and any restrictions your team has given you.

During a long trip

Plan regular chances to get up and walk. When seated, flex your ankles and move your legs.

At work and at home

Break up long periods of sitting and drink water regularly. Raising your legs when resting may ease discomfort.

If your team recommends stockings

Ask how to fit and wear them. Keep taking any prescribed blood thinner as directed, even on days when you are active.

Prenatal visits

Tell your team when symptoms or circumstances change

At each visit, bring questions about symptoms, medicines and anything that has limited your movement. Your team can adjust how often you need to be seen.

Routine pregnancy care

Your team checks your blood pressure and asks how you and your baby are doing. New pain or swelling in one leg needs assessment before the next visit.

When extra scans help

Your clinician may suggest growth scans based on your pregnancy. The link between Factor V Leiden and placental problems, including preeclampsia, is less certain than its link to blood clots.

Labor and birth

Make a written plan before labor starts

Most people with Factor V Leiden can plan for a full-term vaginal birth. Your obstetric team will discuss any separate reason for earlier delivery or a cesarean.

Before your due date

If you use a blood thinner, ask when to hold it for a planned birth. The time needed before an epidural or spinal depends on the medicine and dose.

If labor starts or bleeding begins

Call the birth unit right away, before taking another injection. Tell them the medicine, dose and exact time of your last injection.

After birth

Your team checks bleeding and coordinates with anesthesia before telling you when to restart. Ask for that instruction in writing.

After birth

Before going home, confirm your postpartum plan

Clot prevention still matters once your baby is born. Ask your team to review your delivery, bleeding and current risks with you before discharge.

Before discharge

If medicine is prescribed, write down your first dose at home and stop date. Some people need six weeks of prevention. After a cesarean, SMFM suggests six weeks of medication for patients with inherited clotting conditions such as Factor V Leiden.

As you recover

Walk as soon as your team says you can. Move your ankles while resting and drink water regularly. Heparin is compatible with breastfeeding.

At follow-up

Confirm how long to continue treatment and who handles refills. If you develop warning signs, seek help immediately, even if you are taking heparin.

Your family

Your child can inherit the gene change and stay healthy

Each parent passes on one copy of the factor V gene. The chances below describe what a child inherits from the parent with Factor V Leiden.

1 in 2

Parent with one changed copy

In each pregnancy, there is a 1 in 2 chance that this parent passes on the changed copy.

Every child

Parent with two changed copies

Each child inherits one changed copy from this parent. The other parent contributes the child’s second copy.

Should relatives be tested?

Most children with the change never develop a clot in childhood. Testing healthy relatives is an individual decision. Ask whether a result would change their care.

Questions to ask your MFM specialist
