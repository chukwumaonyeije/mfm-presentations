# Prenatal Diagnosis of Gastroschisis

Audience: Patients and families after a suspected or confirmed prenatal diagnosis of gastroschisis

Format: OpenMFM Presentation Standard v1.0 patient presentation

Status: Outline for approval
Planned length: 17 slides

## Educational design

- Single most important message: Gastroschisis is visible before birth, treatable after birth, and best managed through a coordinated plan that connects prenatal monitoring, delivery at a surgical center, NICU care, and pediatric surgery.
- Communication job: By the end, patients should understand what gastroschisis is, how the diagnosis is confirmed, what ultrasound can and cannot predict, how birth is planned, and what their baby may experience after delivery.
- Common misconceptions: The patient caused the condition; the baby's stomach is outside; gastroschisis and omphalocele are the same; every bowel change on ultrasound proves severe injury; cesarean birth is always required; earlier delivery always protects the bowel; surgery means the baby will feed normally immediately.
- Prerequisite knowledge: None. “Abdominal wall,” “umbilical cord,” “bowel,” “silo,” “NICU,” and “complex gastroschisis” will be defined in plain language.
- Key decisions: Confirming the anatomy; reviewing the rest of the fetal survey; choosing the surveillance plan; selecting a hospital with NICU and pediatric surgery support; individualizing delivery timing; determining mode of birth for obstetric reasons; choosing immediate versus staged closure after birth.
- Six-month memory: “This was not my fault. The prenatal team watches growth and well-being, the pediatric surgery team decides how to repair the opening after birth, and most liveborn babies survive with specialized care.”
- Visual language: Cinematic dark OpenMFM canvas; cyan for anatomy and diagnostic certainty; violet for prenatal monitoring; soft green for coordinated care and recovery; amber for uncertainty; rose reserved for urgent warning signs. Use respectful medical illustration, simplified ultrasound motifs, timelines, and one continuous care pathway. Avoid graphic neonatal photography.
- Source-material use: The 2022 PowerPoint contributes its normal-abdomen comparison, gastroschisis-versus-omphalocele teaching frame, prenatal ultrasound orientation, and staged-repair concept. The pasted notes contribute the topic inventory. Neither source controls the new design or overrides current evidence.

## Slide outline

### 1. Gastroschisis can be seen before birth and treated after birth

- Objective: Introduce the diagnosis with calm realism and a clear promise.
- Key teaching point: Prenatal diagnosis gives the family and care team time to prepare for birth, NICU care, and surgery.
- Visual concept: A luminous fetal profile connected by one cyan pathway to ultrasound, delivery hospital, NICU, and home.
- Anticipated references: ISUOG patient information; CDC gastroschisis overview.

### 2. The opening is beside the belly button

- Objective: Define gastroschisis in plain language.
- Key teaching point: The baby's intestines pass through an opening in the abdominal wall, usually to the right of a normally attached umbilical cord, without a protective sac.
- Visual concept: Respectful simplified anatomy showing the cord, belly button, small opening, and uncovered bowel; “stomach” is explicitly separated from “intestines.”
- Anticipated references: CDC; Feldkamp et al., state-of-the-art review; ERNICA guideline.

### 3. This was not caused by something you did

- Objective: Address guilt early.
- Key teaching point: Gastroschisis develops early in pregnancy, and the cause is usually unknown. A risk factor is not proof of cause in an individual pregnancy.
- Visual concept: Large “Not your fault” statement with a quiet early-development timeline and crossed-out blame language.
- Anticipated references: CDC gastroschisis overview; CDC birth-defects surveillance manual.

### 4. Ultrasound usually makes the diagnosis clear

- Objective: Explain how the prenatal diagnosis is established.
- Key teaching point: Ultrasound shows bowel floating outside the abdomen next to the umbilical cord without a covering sac; a screening blood test may raise suspicion, but ultrasound confirms the anatomy.
- Visual concept: Stylized ultrasound field with three labeled anchors: bowel outside, opening beside the cord, no sac.
- Anticipated references: Feldkamp et al.; CDC; 2025 review from diagnosis to delivery.

### 5. Gastroschisis and omphalocele are different conditions

- Objective: Clarify the main differential diagnosis without overwhelming the audience.
- Key teaching point: Gastroschisis is beside a normally inserted cord and has no sac; omphalocele is centered at the cord and is usually covered. The distinction changes testing, counseling, and newborn planning.
- Visual concept: Side-by-side silhouette comparison using only three visual differences: location, cord insertion, and covering.
- Anticipated references: CDC abdominal-wall-defects manual; ISUOG gastroschisis and omphalocele patient information; source PowerPoint slides 3, 8, 9, and 11.

### 6. A detailed scan looks beyond the abdominal wall

- Objective: Explain what happens after the initial finding.
- Key teaching point: The MFM team confirms the diagnosis, checks fetal anatomy and growth, reviews the bowel and amniotic fluid, and discusses whether additional heart or genetic evaluation fits the whole picture.
- Visual concept: A focused ultrasound sweep that widens from the abdominal wall to the whole baby, ending in a personalized testing conversation.
- Anticipated references: ISUOG patient information; ERNICA guideline; Feldkamp et al.

### 7. Most cases are isolated, but the bowel needs close attention

- Objective: Put associated findings into perspective.
- Key teaching point: Gastroschisis is usually not part of a chromosome syndrome. The main concern is whether the bowel remains healthy or develops a problem such as narrowing, blockage, twisting, or injury.
- Visual concept: The bowel as the primary focal point, with distant genetic and non-bowel findings shown as uncommon rather than impossible.
- Anticipated references: CDC surveillance manual; Feldkamp et al.; ISUOG patient information.

### 8. Follow-up watches growth, fluid, bowel, and well-being

- Objective: Make prenatal surveillance understandable.
- Key teaching point: Repeat ultrasound and later pregnancy testing help the team follow fetal growth, amniotic fluid, bowel appearance, blood flow when indicated, movement, and overall well-being. The exact schedule is individualized.
- Visual concept: Four checkpoints around a calm pregnancy timeline: growth, fluid, bowel, well-being.
- Anticipated references: ISUOG patient information; ERNICA guideline; Chabra et al. prenatal care pathway.

### 9. Ultrasound guides preparation, but it cannot predict every detail

- Objective: Set realistic expectations for prognostic imaging.
- Key teaching point: Some patterns, especially bowel dilation inside the abdomen or excess amniotic fluid, can raise concern for complex gastroschisis. No single measurement can promise exactly what surgery or recovery will be needed.
- Visual concept: A probability dial rather than a pass/fail test, with multiple ultrasound findings combining into one clinical picture.
- Anticipated references: ERNICA guideline; Ferreira et al. systematic review of ultrasound markers; Feldkamp et al.

### 10. The best birth plan connects obstetrics and newborn surgery

- Objective: Explain why place of birth matters.
- Key teaching point: Delivery is usually planned at a hospital with maternal care, neonatology, NICU support, and pediatric surgery so the baby can be stabilized and treated without an avoidable transfer.
- Visual concept: Four teams forming a protective circle around parent and baby, with one shared plan at the center.
- Anticipated references: ISUOG patient information; ERNICA guideline; APSA Fetal Handbook.

### 11. Timing is individualized, and routine early delivery is not the answer

- Objective: Correct the belief that earlier birth always protects the bowel.
- Key teaching point: For uncomplicated gastroschisis, current evidence supports planning birth after 37 weeks, often within a 37-to-39-week window. The exact day depends on fetal testing, growth, bowel findings, labor, and the hospital's coordinated plan.
- Visual concept: A balance scale between continued pregnancy and prematurity, settling into a highlighted 37-to-39-week planning window.
- Anticipated references: APSA 2024 systematic review; ERNICA guideline; Chabra et al.; Feldkamp et al.

### 12. Vaginal birth is usually an option

- Objective: Explain route of delivery in patient-centered terms.
- Key teaching point: Gastroschisis alone is not a reason for cesarean birth. Cesarean delivery is reserved for the usual obstetric reasons or an individualized concern identified by the treating team.
- Visual concept: Two delivery paths with the vaginal path marked “usually possible” and the cesarean path marked “when medically indicated.”
- Anticipated references: ISUOG patient information; ERNICA guideline; APSA Fetal Handbook; Feldkamp et al.

### 13. The first minutes protect the bowel and keep the baby stable

- Objective: Prepare families for the delivery-room sequence.
- Key teaching point: The newborn team protects the exposed bowel, prevents heat and fluid loss, decompresses the stomach, starts IV support, and moves the baby to the NICU for surgical assessment.
- Visual concept: A five-step delivery-room pathway using non-graphic icons: protect, warm, decompress, support, transfer.
- Anticipated references: CDC; APSA Fetal Handbook; ERNICA guideline; Feldkamp et al.

### 14. Repair may happen at once or gradually with a silo

- Objective: Explain the two broad closure strategies.
- Key teaching point: If the bowel fits safely, the surgeon may return it to the abdomen and close the opening. If more time or space is needed, a clear pouch called a silo lets the bowel move inward gradually before closure.
- Visual concept: Two gentle animated-style pathways, “one-stage closure” and “staged silo reduction,” converging on a closed abdominal wall.
- Anticipated references: APSA 2024 systematic review; ERNICA guideline; CDC; source PowerPoint slide 10.

### 15. Feeding starts when the bowel is ready

- Objective: Set expectations for NICU recovery.
- Key teaching point: The bowel often needs time to wake up after repair. Nutrition begins through an IV, milk feeds start gradually, and discharge follows when the baby can grow, maintain temperature, and feed safely.
- Visual concept: A recovery arc from bowel rest to small milk feeds to full feeding and home, with timing intentionally shown as variable.
- Anticipated references: CDC; Feldkamp et al.; ERNICA guideline.

### 16. “Simple” and “complex” describe the bowel, not the baby's worth

- Objective: Explain prognosis honestly without turning labels into destiny.
- Key teaching point: Simple gastroschisis means the bowel is continuous and without major injury. Complex gastroschisis includes a blockage, perforation, loss of blood supply, or another bowel complication and usually means more procedures, nutrition support, and time in the hospital. Most liveborn babies survive with specialized care, but recovery varies.
- Visual concept: One shared starting point branching into a shorter and a longer recovery path, both surrounded by the same care team.
- Anticipated references: Feldkamp et al.; ERNICA guideline; APSA 2024 systematic review; 2025 review from diagnosis to delivery.

### 17. Bring these questions to your MFM and pediatric surgery teams

- Objective: Close with practical agency, reassurance, and required patient safeguards.
- Key teaching point: Families should leave with a diagnosis they understand, a surveillance schedule, a delivery location and timing plan, a newborn surgery plan, and clear instructions for urgent concerns.
- Visual concept: Printable question pathway grouped into “pregnancy,” “birth,” “surgery,” and “going home,” followed by a concise educational disclaimer and privacy statement.
- Questions: What confirms the diagnosis? Are there any other findings? What will each follow-up visit check? Which ultrasound changes would alter the plan? Where should I deliver? What timing range do you recommend for me? Is vaginal birth appropriate? Can I meet neonatology and pediatric surgery before birth? How might one-stage closure and a silo differ? What should I expect for feeding and length of stay? Who should I call for decreased fetal movement, labor, bleeding, fluid leakage, or another urgent concern?
- Educational disclaimer: This presentation supports informed discussion. It cannot diagnose a pregnancy, predict an individual baby's course, choose delivery timing, or replace care from the patient's own clinical team.
- Privacy: This deck collects no patient information and uses no analytics or external forms.
- Anticipated references: ISUOG patient information; ERNICA guideline; CDC; APSA 2024 systematic review.

## Evidence decisions for the HTML phase

- Present the 37-to-39-week range as guidance for uncomplicated cases, not a fixed promise or universal mandate.
- Do not state that progressive bowel dilation, edema, or polyhydramnios automatically requires early cesarean delivery. These findings prompt reassessment and individualized planning; evidence does not support that categorical rule.
- Do not prescribe neonatal fluid volumes, antibiotic regimens, ventilation thresholds, or operative parameters in a patient deck.
- Describe prenatal ultrasound markers as risk signals with imperfect prediction.
- Keep outcome language anchored to liveborn infants receiving specialized care, and avoid implying that every baby follows the same NICU course.
