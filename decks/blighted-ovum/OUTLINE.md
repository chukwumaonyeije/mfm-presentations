# Blighted Ovum: Understanding an Empty-Sac Early Pregnancy Loss

Audience: Patients with a suspected or confirmed empty-sac early pregnancy loss, and their support people

Format: OpenMFM Presentation Standard v1.0 patient presentation

Status: Outline for approval
Planned length: 16 slides

## Educational design

- Single most important message: An empty gestational sac on one early ultrasound does not always prove a pregnancy loss. A careful diagnosis protects a potentially early pregnancy, and once loss is confirmed, most medically stable patients can choose among waiting, medication, or uterine aspiration.
- Communication job: By the end, patients should understand what the finding means, why confirmation may take time, which symptoms require urgent care, and how to choose a management plan that fits their medical needs and preferences.
- Common misconceptions: The patient caused the loss; an empty sac always means the scan was definitive; one hCG value can prove location or viability; more blood tests are always better; surgery is always required; one early loss predicts infertility; every RhD-negative patient must receive Rh immune globulin before 12 weeks.
- Prerequisite knowledge: None. “Gestational sac,” “embryo,” “transvaginal ultrasound,” “hCG,” and “ectopic pregnancy” will be defined in plain language.
- Key decisions: Whether the pregnancy location is established; whether the ultrasound is definitive or requires repeat imaging; whether symptoms make urgent treatment necessary; which management option best fits the patient; how completion will be confirmed; whether Rh testing or Rh immune globulin should be discussed under current local guidance.
- Six-month memory: “A careful diagnosis comes first. If the loss is confirmed and I am medically stable, I usually have choices.”
- Visual language: Cinematic dark OpenMFM canvas; cyan for the diagnostic pathway; violet for uncertainty and repeat assessment; soft green for supported choices; rose reserved for urgent warning signs. Use a calm ultrasound-inspired circle motif, timelines, and simple pathways rather than fetal imagery or realistic pregnancy-loss tissue.
- Tone: Direct, compassionate, nonjudgmental, and low anxiety. Use “early pregnancy loss with an empty gestational sac” as the preferred description while acknowledging that patients may hear “blighted ovum” or “anembryonic pregnancy.”

## Slide outline

### 1. An empty sac deserves a careful answer

- Objective: Establish the presentation’s promise and acknowledge the emotional weight of the finding.
- Key teaching point: The first task is to determine whether the ultrasound is simply early or whether it confirms an early pregnancy loss.
- Visual concept: A single luminous gestational-sac circle at the center of a dark field, opening into two quiet paths labeled “too early to know” and “loss confirmed.”
- Anticipated references: ACOG Early Pregnancy Loss FAQ; ACOG Practice Bulletin No. 200; SRU first-trimester ultrasound lexicon (2024).

### 2. “Blighted ovum” is an older name for an empty-sac loss

- Objective: Define the topic without using language that blames the pregnancy or the patient.
- Key teaching point: The pregnancy implanted and formed a gestational sac, but an embryo did not become visible. Current ultrasound language favors “early pregnancy loss with an empty gestational sac.”
- Visual concept: Three terms arranged as a language transition: “blighted ovum” → “anembryonic pregnancy” → “early pregnancy loss with an empty gestational sac.”
- Anticipated references: SRU/SMFM/ACOG first-trimester ultrasound lexicon (2024); ACOG Practice Bulletin No. 200.

### 3. This was not caused by something you did

- Objective: Address guilt before explaining technical details.
- Key teaching point: Early pregnancy loss is common, and many losses are related to chance chromosome differences. Work, ordinary exercise, sex, stress, or an argument do not cause miscarriage.
- Visual concept: A large “Not your fault” statement surrounded by gently crossed-out self-blame myths.
- Anticipated references: ACOG Early Pregnancy Loss FAQ; ACOG Practice Bulletin No. 200.

### 4. One empty-looking sac can represent different moments

- Objective: Explain why a single early scan may not be the final answer.
- Key teaching point: The scan may be earlier than expected, may show a confirmed empty-sac loss, or may not yet establish where the pregnancy is located.
- Visual concept: Three parallel ultrasound timelines: dates earlier than expected, confirmed intrauterine loss, and pregnancy of unknown location.
- Anticipated references: ACOG Practice Bulletin No. 200; NICE NG126; SRU diagnostic criteria and lexicon.

### 5. Ultrasound carries most of the diagnostic weight

- Objective: Explain the primary diagnostic tool in patient-friendly language.
- Key teaching point: Transvaginal ultrasound looks for the sac, yolk sac, embryo, and cardiac activity and measures what is visible. Strict size and time criteria prevent treatment of a pregnancy that may still be developing.
- Visual concept: A labeled, non-photorealistic ultrasound field with four sequential landmarks and a measurement caliper.
- Anticipated references: ACOG Practice Bulletin No. 200; Doubilet et al., NEJM (2013); SRU lexicon (2024); NICE NG126.

### 6. Repeat imaging is a safety step, not a delay without purpose

- Objective: Prepare patients for an interval scan when the first study is uncertain.
- Key teaching point: If the findings do not meet strict criteria, another transvaginal ultrasound after an appropriate interval provides the safest answer. Calendar dates and last menstrual period alone are not enough.
- Visual concept: A protected interval timeline: first scan → development window → repeat scan → clear decision.
- Anticipated references: ACOG Practice Bulletin No. 200; NICE NG126; Doubilet et al., NEJM (2013).

### 7. Blood tests can guide follow-up, but they do not replace the scan

- Objective: Correct overreliance on hormone values.
- Key teaching point: Serial hCG may help when the pregnancy location or course is unclear, but one hCG value cannot prove where the pregnancy is or whether it will continue. Estradiol is not a routine diagnostic test, and progesterone should not be used to determine location in a pregnancy of unknown location.
- Visual concept: An ultrasound image as the central anchor, with hCG shown as a supporting trend line rather than a pass/fail meter; estradiol and progesterone placed outside the routine pathway.
- Anticipated references: ACOG Practice Bulletin No. 200; NICE NG126 pregnancy-of-unknown-location recommendations.

### 8. The care team must still consider ectopic pregnancy

- Objective: Explain the most safety-critical alternative diagnosis without causing unnecessary alarm.
- Key teaching point: If a definite pregnancy inside the uterus has not been established, follow-up continues until the location is known or the pregnancy resolves. New one-sided pain, shoulder pain, weakness, dizziness, or fainting requires urgent evaluation.
- Visual concept: A simple uterus-and-tubes location map with “inside the uterus confirmed?” as the decision point and a rose emergency path.
- Anticipated references: ACOG Practice Bulletin No. 200; NICE NG126; SRU lexicon (2024).

### 9. Once the diagnosis is certain, the safest plan starts with your condition

- Objective: Introduce management as a shared decision framed by clinical stability.
- Key teaching point: Heavy bleeding, hemodynamic instability, or infection needs urgent procedural care. Otherwise, waiting, medication, and uterine aspiration are all accepted options, and long-term outcomes are similar.
- Visual concept: A triage fork: “urgent medical need” leads to rapid treatment; “medically stable” opens into three equal choice paths.
- Anticipated references: ACOG Practice Bulletin No. 200; ACOG Early Pregnancy Loss FAQ; NICE NG126.

### 10. Waiting lets the body pass the pregnancy naturally

- Objective: Explain expectant management realistically.
- Key teaching point: Waiting avoids medication and a procedure, but the timing is unpredictable and bleeding and cramping may be heavy. A backup plan and follow-up are part of this choice.
- Visual concept: A flexible calendar with three checkpoints: what to expect, when to call, and how completion is confirmed.
- Anticipated references: ACOG Practice Bulletin No. 200; ACOG Early Pregnancy Loss FAQ; RCOG Early Miscarriage patient information; NICE NG126.

### 11. Medication offers more control over timing

- Objective: Explain medical management without turning the slide into self-treatment instructions.
- Key teaching point: Clinician-prescribed mifepristone followed by misoprostol is more effective than misoprostol alone when mifepristone is available. Cramping, bleeding, pain control, and follow-up should be planned before treatment begins.
- Visual concept: A two-step medication timeline beside a “prepare at home” checklist for support person, pain plan, pads, contact number, and follow-up.
- Anticipated references: ACOG Practice Bulletin No. 200; Schreiber et al., NEJM (2018); NICE NG126.

### 12. Uterine aspiration is the fastest, most predictable option

- Objective: Explain procedural management clearly and neutrally.
- Key teaching point: Vacuum aspiration removes the pregnancy tissue through the cervix. It may be preferred for speed and predictability and is recommended urgently for severe bleeding, instability, or infection.
- Visual concept: A calm procedure-day pathway: prepare → aspiration → recovery → follow-up, with no graphic procedural imagery.
- Anticipated references: ACOG Practice Bulletin No. 200; ACOG Early Pregnancy Loss FAQ; RCOG surgical-management patient information.

### 13. Know which symptoms cannot wait

- Objective: Give patients an actionable safety plan.
- Key teaching point: Seek urgent help for fainting or severe weakness, severe or worsening abdominal or one-sided pain, shoulder pain, very heavy bleeding, fever, foul-smelling discharge, breathing difficulty, or feeling dangerously unwell. Follow the specific bleeding threshold and contact plan given by the treating team.
- Visual concept: A high-contrast rose “urgent care” lane and a cyan “call your care team” lane, each with large symptom icons.
- Anticipated references: ACOG Early Pregnancy Loss FAQ; ACOG Practice Bulletin No. 200; NICE NG126; RCOG Early Miscarriage.

### 14. Follow-up confirms recovery, not just an empty ultrasound

- Objective: Set expectations after expectant, medical, or procedural management.
- Key teaching point: Follow-up may use symptoms, ultrasound, a urine pregnancy test, or serial hCG depending on the original findings and treatment. Routine hCG testing until zero is not required for every confirmed intrauterine loss. RhD testing and Rh immune globulin recommendations before 12 weeks now differ across guidelines, so the plan should reflect gestational age, treatment, local policy, and shared decision-making.
- Visual concept: Four follow-up routes converging on “recovery confirmed,” with a small evidence-variation callout for Rh care.
- Anticipated references: ACOG Practice Bulletin No. 200 and 2024 RhD Clinical Practice Update; SMFM RhD statement (2024); NICE NG126, updated June 2026.

### 15. Questions to ask your obstetric or MFM specialist

- Objective: Turn the presentation into a practical visit agenda.
- Key teaching point: Patients should leave with diagnostic certainty, a chosen care plan, an emergency plan, and a clear method of follow-up.
- Visual concept: Eight large printable questions with check circles.
- Anticipated references: Synthesis of cited guidance.
- Questions:
  1. Is this diagnosis certain, or do I need another ultrasound?
  2. Has the pregnancy been definitely located inside the uterus?
  3. What did the sac measure, and which diagnostic criterion was met?
  4. Am I medically stable enough to choose among waiting, medication, and aspiration?
  5. What should I expect for pain, bleeding, and timing with each option?
  6. Exactly when should I call you, and when should I go to emergency care?
  7. How will we confirm that treatment or natural passage is complete?
  8. Do Rh testing or Rh immune globulin recommendations apply to me under your current protocol?

### 16. A careful diagnosis protects you, and your preferences shape the plan

- Objective: Close with reassurance, boundaries, and the durable take-home message.
- Key teaching point: This loss was not the patient’s fault. A confirmed empty-sac loss can usually be managed safely in more than one way, future fertility is generally not changed by the choice among accepted options, and emotional support is part of care.
- Visual concept: A calm horizon with the path “confirm → choose → recover,” followed by three concise take-home statements and expandable references.
- Anticipated references: ACOG Practice Bulletin No. 200; ACOG Early Pregnancy Loss FAQ; RCOG Early Miscarriage; Schreiber et al., NEJM (2018).
- Educational disclaimer: This presentation supports informed discussion. It cannot diagnose a pregnancy, select treatment, or replace urgent evaluation or care from the patient’s own clinician.
- Privacy: The presentation collects no patient information, uses no analytics or external forms, and contains no protected health information.

## Evidence corrections applied to the supplied notes

1. The deck will not call serial beta-hCG, progesterone, and estradiol a standard diagnostic bundle. Ultrasound is primary; serial hCG has a supporting role when location or course is uncertain; estradiol is not routine; and NICE advises against using serum progesterone to diagnose location or viability in a pregnancy of unknown location.
2. The deck will not imply that one ultrasound, one hCG value, or last-menstrual-period dating alone is enough when imaging is equivocal. Conservative size- and time-based criteria plus repeat imaging protect against ending a potentially developing pregnancy.
3. The mean sac diameter threshold of 25 mm without an embryo will be presented as a strict SRU diagnostic criterion, while also explaining that some systems, including NICE, recommend a second opinion and/or repeat scan before diagnosis even at that threshold.
4. Medical treatment will use current evidence: mifepristone 200 mg orally followed by misoprostol 800 micrograms vaginally is more effective than misoprostol alone when available. Repeat-dose timing and route vary by protocol and will remain clinician-directed.
5. The deck will not state that every RhD-negative patient must receive Rh immune globulin for every loss. ACOG updated its guidance in 2024, SMFM recommends offering Rh testing and Rh immune globulin when feasible, and NICE changed its recommendations in June 2026. This will be labeled as an evolving, locally implemented area for shared decision-making.
6. The deck will not mandate histopathology, confirmation of chorionic villi, or serial hCG until zero after every loss. These are selective strategies based on pregnancy location, clinical course, management, local practice, and concern for ectopic or molar pregnancy.
7. Early gestational trophoblastic disease will not be shown as a routine patient-level explanation for every empty sac. It is uncommon and is evaluated when imaging, hCG, symptoms, or pathology raises a specific concern.
8. “Blighted ovum” will be acknowledged for search and patient recognition, but the visible teaching language will favor the more precise and compassionate SRU terminology.
