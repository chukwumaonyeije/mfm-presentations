01 / Understand the finding

Clubfoot turns the foot inward and down

One or both feet may be affected.

1 in 1,000

About this many babies in the US are born with clubfoot.

The medical name is talipes equinovarus .

Atlanta Perinatal Associates Sources [2] [3] 2 / 15
01 / Understand the finding

The cause is often unknown

Family history can play a role

Genes are traits passed down in families. They can affect how the foot forms.

This is not a reason for blame

Finding clubfoot does not mean you did something wrong.

02 / Make a care plan

A scan is the start of the assessment

A baby’s position can sometimes look like clubfoot.

1. Review the scan

Your team takes a closer look at the foot.

2. Talk about the findings

Ask what is clear and what is still unknown.

3. Check after birth

An exam helps confirm the diagnosis.

02 / Make a care plan

Other findings help guide the care plan

A detailed scan checks the rest of your baby.

Foot finding alone

This is called “isolated” clubfoot.

Other findings present

The team may suggest more tests or care.

Ask about genetic counseling and testing. One option is amniocentesis: testing a small sample of fluid around the baby for chromosome changes. Chromosomes carry genetic information. You can discuss the benefits and risks before you choose.

02 / Make a care plan

Clubfoot alone usually does not change birth plans

During pregnancy

The timing and type of birth usually follow your other pregnancy needs.

Before treatment

Meet a children’s foot specialist, called a pediatric orthopedic specialist.

03 / Follow the treatment steps

Treatment happens in steps

This plan is called the Ponseti method .

1. Casts

Gently move the foot over time.

2. Heel-cord care

Often, a small procedure releases a tight cord.

3. A brace

Helps hold the corrected position.

Keep follow-up visits through treatment and as your child grows.

03 / Follow the treatment steps

Each cast gently changes the foot’s position

Treatment starts soon after birth.

A new cast is placed about once a week, often for several weeks.

The number of casts depends on your baby’s foot.

Atlanta Perinatal Associates Sources [2] [3] 8 / 15
03 / Follow the treatment steps

Many babies need a small heel-cord procedure

The heel cord links the calf muscle to the heel.

A small procedure, called a tenotomy , releases the tight cord.

It is often done with numbing medicine. A final cast protects the cord as it heals.

03 / Follow the treatment steps

The brace helps keep the foot straight

After the casts

Often 23 hours a day for the first 3 months.

Then, during sleep

Nights and naps, often until age 4–5.

Follow your specialist’s schedule.

04 / Look ahead

Most treated children can walk, run, and play

An active life

Most children with isolated clubfoot do well with treatment.

Some differences may remain

The foot or calf may stay smaller.

Follow-up still matters

The foot can turn back. Some children need more treatment.

Atlanta Perinatal Associates Sources [2] [3] 11 / 15
04 / Look ahead

A strong plan leaves room for changes

What we know

Casts and braces help many children. A good early result still needs follow-up.

What may vary

The time in treatment and the long-term result differ from child to child.

If brace wear is hard, ask your team for help. The foot can turn back even when families follow the plan.

Atlanta Perinatal Associates Sources [2] [3] 12 / 15
05 / Take the next step

Remember: check, treat, and keep follow-up

Check

Review the scan findings with your team.

Treat

Meet the foot specialist and make a plan.

Keep follow-up

Use the brace as prescribed, even when the foot looks straight.

Why is the brace needed after the casts?

It helps keep the foot in its new position and lowers the chance of it turning back.
