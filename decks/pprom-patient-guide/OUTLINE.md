# Your Water Broke Early: PPROM at 27 Weeks

*A bedside guide for a patient who is in the hospital right now.*

Status: outline presented to the user in session; user approved as-is and elected to proceed directly to production, publication and deploy. Implemented as `index.html` under OpenMFM Presentation Standard v1.0. The user also elected to leave the two unverified figures out of the deck (see editorial boundary 2).
Prepared: September 2026. Evidence check performed at time of writing; not a systematic literature search.
Audience: a patient with preterm prelabor rupture of membranes (PPROM) at 27 weeks who is currently admitted to hospital. Companion to the existing provider deck `decks/pprom-doctors`.
Folder: `decks/pprom-patient-guide/`

## Educational design

- **Audience.** A pregnant patient at 27 0/7 weeks who has been told her water broke early and that she is staying in hospital. She is anxious, possibly on bed rest, reading on a phone or tablet, and interrupted frequently by staff. No prior medical knowledge assumed. Some readers will be her partner or family.
- **Central message.** *Your water breaking early is why you are in the hospital — not a punishment and not a verdict. The plan from here is to keep your baby inside as long as it is safe for both of you, and to deliver deliberately, with medicines already on board, rather than suddenly.*
- **Duration.** 24 slides, about 25–30 minutes read straight through; designed mainly for selective re-reading across a multi-day admission. Every slide fits a desktop viewport at 1568×904; the densest reference slide scrolls slightly.
- **Prerequisites.** None. Membrane, amniotic fluid, gestational age, cervix, chorioamnionitis, latency, corticosteroid, magnesium sulfate, GBS and NICU are each defined before use.
- **Misconceptions addressed.**
  1. "My water broke, so the baby has to be born now."
  2. "The doctors will keep checking me internally to see how things are going."
  3. "Antibiotics are because I did something wrong / because I have an infection."
  4. "The steroid shot is for me, or it only helps if I deliver soon."
  5. "Magnesium is a treatment for the pregnancy that everyone gets."
  6. "If labor starts, they will stop it."
  7. "Once the water breaks, the baby runs out of fluid and cannot survive."
  8. "Being on the monitor all day means something is wrong."
  9. "The fluid keeps leaking, so I must be doing something to make it worse."
  10. "34 weeks is a normal, full-term delivery." (It is not; it is the point at which the balance tips.)
- **Key decisions the patient faces.** Remaining in hospital for the duration; accepting the antibiotic regimen and GBS prophylaxis; accepting antenatal corticosteroids and magnesium sulfate; accepting or declining the recommended swabs and blood tests; how much activity is safe; where and how she will deliver; and consent for a possible cesarean if cord prolapse or fetal compromise occurs.
- **Concepts that need diagrams rather than text.** (a) The membranes, the bag of water and the cervix, with and without a rupture; (b) the three hazards of PPROM — ascending infection, cord prolapse, placental abruption; (c) the three medicines and what each one is for; (d) the daily surveillance loop; (e) the 27 → 34 week timeline with what changes at 34; (f) warning signs as a bedside checklist.
- **Six-month takeaway.** She should be able to say: why she was admitted and why she stayed; which three medicines she was given and what each one was for; that internal examinations were deliberately avoided; the daily checks to expect; the specific warning signs to report immediately; that the plan was delivery at 34 weeks if everything stayed stable; and that developing a fever is the single most important thing to report.

## Provenance and editorial boundaries

**Source material.** The clinician supplied a detailed provider-level summary of PPROM management at 27 weeks, including the NICHD and contemporary antibiotic regimens, antenatal corticosteroids, magnesium sulfate neuroprotection, inpatient expectant management to 34 0/7 weeks, latency data, delivery indications, cerclage management and investigational therapies. Its own reference list was empty.

**Editorial boundaries applied.**

1. **The deck is re-authored for the patient, not down-converted.** Dose-level prescribing detail, alternative regimens for penicillin allergy, cerclage-retention controversy and investigational therapies (amnioinfusion, fetoscopic sealants, probiotics) are out of scope for a patient at the bedside and are already covered in `decks/pprom-doctors`. What survives is the patient-relevant 20%: why she is here, what each medicine is for, what we watch, what she must report, and when delivery is planned.
2. **Two statistics from the source were not used.** The supplied claim of a live-birth rate of about 66% at 27 weeks and a mean latency of about 39.4 days carried no citation, and the source's reference list was empty. Neither figure could be verified against a citable source at the time of writing, so neither appears in the deck. Latency is instead described qualitatively ("days to weeks; earlier ruptures usually give more time, later ones less"), and the deck tells the reader that her own team will give her the numbers that apply to her.
3. **Unreferenced provider claims were re-grounded or dropped.** Every clinical statement in the deck is tied to R1–R14. Where the source described "emerging strategies" and "limited RCT data on outpatient care," those are provider-facing controversies and are omitted rather than simplified into a patient claim.
4. **Fear figures are named, not softened.** Cord prolapse, abruption, infection and preterm birth are stated plainly, with what would be done about each, because a patient who does not know the warning signs cannot report them.
5. **No outcome promise is made.** The deck says that a baby born at 27 weeks and a baby born at 34 weeks have very different needs, without quoting survival percentages that would depend on her unit and her baby.
6. **The 34-week plan is presented as US guidance.** ACOG PB 217 sets 34 0/7 weeks; the deck notes that this is the plan *if* everything stays stable, and that the timing is the team's decision based on her situation.
7. **Privacy.** No patient-identifying information appears. No case, measurement or identifier from any real patient is reproduced; the audience is described rather than illustrated.
8. **Clinical review status stays explicit.** The deck is labelled as patient education with clinical review pending, and does not imply clinician sign-off.
