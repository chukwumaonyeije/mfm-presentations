A Personalized Care Plan

BMI is one part of your health. It helps your team plan safer, more comfortable care for you and your baby.

Risk in perspective

Some risks are higher. Your outcome is not predetermined.

For you

  • Gestational diabetes
  • High blood pressure and preeclampsia
  • Sleep apnea and anesthesia challenges
  • Cesarean birth, infection, bleeding, or blood clots

For your baby

  • Growth that is larger or smaller than expected
  • Preterm birth
  • A higher chance of some birth differences
  • A higher risk of stillbirth as BMI and gestational age rise
i
Association is not destiny.

Most patients with obesity give birth to healthy babies. Added planning helps find problems early and reduce preventable harm.

Source: ACOG Practice Bulletin No. 230, reaffirmed 2025.

Diabetes screening

Early diabetes testing and routine GDM screening answer different questions.

Before 15 weeks

Was diabetes present before pregnancy?

Your clinician may use standard diabetes tests when your history or risk profile suggests testing is appropriate.

→
24–28 weeks

Has gestational diabetes developed?

Patients not already diagnosed with diabetes complete routine GDM screening during this window.

!
Important distinction

The 75-g GDM thresholds of 92, 180, and 153 mg/dL were developed for 24–28 weeks. ADA advises against using them as an early-pregnancy GDM test.

Source: American Diabetes Association. Standards of Care in Diabetes—2026, Sections 2 and 15.

Sleep and breathing

Sleep apnea deserves early attention.

Tell your team
  • Loud or frequent snoring
  • Witnessed pauses in breathing or gasping
  • Morning headaches or unrefreshing sleep
  • Severe daytime fatigue
  • Known sleep apnea or current CPAP use
What happens next

Your team may recommend sleep testing when symptoms and clinical risk are high. If sleep apnea is confirmed, treatment such as CPAP informs anesthesia and postpartum monitoring.

i
Screening is a conversation, not a label.

BMI increases risk, but symptoms, health history, and access to testing determine the next step.

Source: SASM/SOAP Consensus Guideline on Obstructive Sleep Apnea in Pregnancy, 2023.

Fetal growth

Growth monitoring should be individualized.

After 24 weeks

Start with the clinical assessment

Fundal height remains the usual screening tool when it can be measured reliably.

→
Add ultrasound when useful

Measure growth when the answer will change care

Ultrasound may replace fundal height when measurement is not feasible or when another maternal or fetal indication exists.

i
There is no universal every-four-week rule.

Your scan schedule should reflect image quality, fetal growth, diabetes, hypertension, prior history, and local practice.

Source: ACOG Clinical Consensus No. 8, Tailored Prenatal Care Delivery, 2025.

Nutrition and movement

Healthy gain, not weight-loss dieting, is the pregnancy goal.

  • Build meals around vegetables or fruit, protein, fiber-rich carbohydrates, and water.
  • Choose activity you can sustain, such as walking, swimming, or prenatal strength work, when your clinician says it is safe.
  • Take a prenatal vitamin with at least 400 micrograms of folic acid unless your clinician prescribes another dose.
  • Track health behaviors and fetal growth, not only the number on the scale.
11–20
The target is a guide.

If gain is below the range but fetal growth is appropriate, forcing weight gain has not been shown to improve outcomes.

Sources: ACOG Committee Opinion No. 548; ACOG Physical Activity and Exercise During Pregnancy and the Postpartum Period.

After bariatric surgery

Pregnancy after bariatric surgery needs a different plan.

Before conception

Allow weight to stabilize

ACOG advises delaying pregnancy for 12–24 months after surgery, during the period of fastest weight loss.

During pregnancy

Monitor nutrition

Your team may check iron, folate, vitamin B12, calcium, vitamin D, and other nutrients based on the procedure.

Glucose testing

Avoid preventable symptoms

Some patients cannot tolerate a glucose drink after gastric bypass. Your clinician can choose an alternative strategy.

!
Severe or persistent abdominal pain, vomiting, or inability to eat needs prompt evaluation.

Pregnancy does not protect against surgical complications.

Sources: ACOG Interpregnancy Care; ACOG Practice Bulletin No. 105.

Labor and delivery

Delivery planning starts before labor.

Anesthesia

An antenatal consultation may help with airway, IV access, pain relief, sleep apnea, and medications.

Labor

Labor may take longer. Early epidural placement may be discussed, but the plan remains individualized.

Birth route

Many patients deliver vaginally. Obesity alone is not an indication for cesarean birth.

Timing

Induction at 39 weeks may be offered in appropriate settings. It is not mandatory solely because of BMI.

39
No elective delivery before 39 weeks.

Earlier delivery is reserved for a medical or obstetric indication. Your clinician should explain the reason and alternatives.

Sources: ACOG Practice Bulletin No. 230; ACOG Clinical Practice Guideline No. 8; ACOG Committee Opinion No. 765.

Postpartum care

Risk prevention continues after birth.

Before leaving the hospital
  • Walk as soon as it is safe.
  • After cesarean birth, use sequential compression devices until fully mobile.
  • Use anticoagulant medication when your total VTE risk and hospital protocol support it.
  • Review blood pressure, wound, breathing, and pain concerns.
After discharge
  • Ask for lactation support early.
  • If you had GDM, complete a 75-g OGTT 4–12 weeks postpartum.
  • Continue diabetes screening every 1–3 years after GDM.
  • Build a sustainable interpregnancy health plan.
!
Get urgent help for chest pain, trouble breathing, one-sided leg pain or swelling, severe headache, vision change, heavy bleeding, or thoughts of harming yourself or your baby.

Sources: SMFM Consult Series No. 51, reaffirmed 2024; ADA Standards of Care in Diabetes—2026.

Prepare for your visit

Bring these questions to your MFM specialist.

Ask your team
  • What weight-gain range fits my pregnancy?
  • Do I need early diabetes or sleep-apnea testing?
  • What is my anatomy and growth ultrasound plan?
  • Should I have NST or BPP testing, and when?
  • Would an anesthesia consultation help me?
  • How will we prevent blood clots after birth?
Remember
  • BMI informs care. It does not predict your outcome.
  • Early diabetes testing is not the routine GDM test.
  • Ultrasound and fetal testing schedules are individualized.
  • Healthy gain is the goal. Pregnancy is not the time for restrictive dieting.
  • Delivery and postpartum plans should be made before labor.
✓
You are the central member of the care team.

Clear questions and shared decisions are part of excellent maternal-fetal medicine.
