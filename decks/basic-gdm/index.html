01 / Understand

Pregnancy makes insulin
work less effectively.

Glucose is sugar in your blood. Insulin helps your body use it for energy.

  1. 01

    Placenta

    Pregnancy hormones increase insulin resistance.

  2. 02

    Pancreas

    Your body needs to make more insulin.

  3. 03

    GDM

    If it cannot keep up, blood glucose rises.

GDM is a health condition, not a personal failure.

01 / Understand

Risk factors matter.
Screening still matters.

GDM can occur even without obvious risk factors.

Your history

Prior GDM, a previous large baby, or a close relative with diabetes.

Your health

PCOS, higher body weight, increasing age, or high blood pressure.

Routine testing helps find GDM that has no symptoms.

Clinician context
Risk varies across populations and reflects biological, social, and health-care factors. Risk factors guide early assessment for unrecognized diabetes; they do not replace routine GDM screening. PCOS means polycystic ovary syndrome.

01 / Understand

Treatment lowers important
pregnancy risks.

The goal is a healthier pregnancy, not a perfect glucose log.

For you

Reduce the risk of pregnancy-related high blood pressure and some delivery complications.

For your baby

Reduce excessive growth and shoulder dystocia: difficulty delivering the shoulders.

After birth, babies may need extra support for low blood glucose.

What the evidence shows
Randomized trials support a package of nutrition counseling, glucose monitoring, follow-up, and insulin when needed. ACHOIS reduced serious perinatal complications. In the MFMU mild-GDM trial, the primary composite did not significantly improve, but excessive growth, shoulder dystocia, cesarean delivery, and hypertensive disorders were reduced. These trials do not prove one medication is best for every outcome.

02 / Testing

Most GDM testing happens
at 24–28 weeks.

Earlier testing answers a different question: was diabetes already present?

  1. 01

    Early pregnancy

    Assess for previously unrecognized diabetes when indicated.

  2. 02

    24–28 weeks

    Use the practice’s standard GDM testing pathway.

A normal early test does not replace later GDM testing.

Clinician context
Overt diabetes may be identified by fasting plasma glucose ≥126 mg/dL or A1C ≥6.5%, with confirmation when appropriate. Mild early hyperglycemia below overt-diabetes criteria has a less settled treatment pathway. Do not automatically apply later-pregnancy GDM thresholds early.

02 / Testing

An abnormal screen usually
means another test.

The common U.S. two-step pathway starts with a screening drink.

  1. 01

    50-g screen

    No fasting. Blood drawn after 1 hour.

  2. 02

    If screen is elevated

    The practice uses a cutoff of 130, 135, or 140 mg/dL.

  3. 03

    100-g diagnostic test

    Fast beforehand. Blood drawn fasting and at 1, 2, and 3 hours.

The first test screens for risk; the next test usually establishes the diagnosis.

02 / Testing

Some practices use
one diagnostic test.

The one-step pathway uses a fasting 75-g glucose drink.

  1. 01

    Before the drink

    Measure fasting glucose.

  2. 02

    After the drink

    Measure at 1 hour and 2 hours.

  3. 03

    Interpret the results

    One value at or above its threshold establishes GDM.

Follow the preparation instructions for the test your practice orders.

02 / Testing

The diagnosis rule
depends on the test.

These laboratory cutoffs diagnose GDM. They are not your home glucose goals.

100-g test · two-step

Usually 2 or more abnormal values.

75-g test · one-step

1 or more abnormal values.

Ask which pathway was used before interpreting a result.

02 / Testing

A1C does not replace
the glucose tolerance test.

A1C reflects average glucose over time. Pregnancy can affect its interpretation.

Useful context

It can help identify previously unrecognized diabetes early.

Daily decisions

Fasting and postmeal readings guide the GDM treatment plan.

A reassuring A1C does not rule out GDM.

03 / Daily care

Start with three
practical tools.

Your team will help you build a plan that fits your life.

Food

Individual nutrition counseling.

Movement

Activity that is safe for your pregnancy.

Monitoring

A meter or agreed device, supplies, and a review plan.

Ask who reviews your readings and how to contact them.

What A1GDM and A2GDM mean
A1GDM describes glucose controlled without medication. A2GDM describes GDM requiring medication. Both need glucose monitoring and obstetric follow-up. These labels describe treatment needs, not personal success.

03 / Daily care

Check fasting glucose
and one postmeal time.

A common starting routine is four checks each day.

  1. 01

    Morning

    Before breakfast, after the overnight fast.

  2. 02

    After each meal

    At 1 hour OR 2 hours, as your team directs.

  3. 03

    Share the pattern

    Record readings, timing, and relevant food or medication changes.

Agree on when the postmeal clock starts, usually the first bite.

03 / Daily care

A sensor adds trends.
You still need a plan.

A continuous glucose monitor (CGM) measures glucose in fluid under the skin.

What it adds

Shows patterns between meals and overnight.

What to check

Use a fingerstick when symptoms disagree with the sensor or your device instructions require it.

Ask which readings should guide treatment and when to confirm them.

Clinician context
Tissue and blood glucose can differ, especially during rapid changes. CGM evidence is strongest for type 1 diabetes in pregnancy; GDM use should be individualized. A sensor does not replace the diagnostic glucose tolerance test.

03 / Daily care

Repeated high readings
mean the plan needs adjusting.

Needing medication is not a failure.

  1. 01

    Notice

    Is fasting glucose high? Is one meal repeatedly above target?

  2. 02

    Share

    Send readings on the schedule your team gives you.

  3. 03

    Adjust

    Review food, timing, and medication needs together.

Do not wait for the next routine visit if readings remain above your agreed limits.

Follow-up and escalation
Review commonly occurs within about 1–2 weeks of starting nutrition therapy, sooner with marked elevations or advancing gestation. There is no universal percentage of elevated readings that mandates medication. Establish individualized call thresholds and sick-day instructions.

04 / Medication

Insulin can target
the times glucose is high.

Insulin is the preferred medication in ADA and ACOG guidance when lifestyle measures are not enough.

Fasting elevations

Background insulin can address overnight glucose.

After-meal elevations

Mealtime insulin can address a specific meal.

Your team teaches the dose, timing, injection technique, and low-glucose plan.

Clinician context
Insulin does not cross the placenta in clinically significant amounts. Regimens and titration depend on the glucose pattern, gestational age, weight, meals, and hypoglycemia risk. Needs can change as pregnancy progresses. Do not use a generic starting dose as an individual prescription.

04 / Medication

Discuss the tradeoffs
of medication choices.

An oral medicine may be considered after counseling.

Metformin

Crosses the placenta. Some patients still need insulin; long-term offspring outcomes remain a consideration.

Glyburide

Generally not a first-line choice because of less favorable newborn outcomes.

Tell your team about cost, access, injection concerns, and side effects.

Why guideline recommendations differ
ADA/ACOG prefer insulin. SMFM’s statement, reaffirmed in 2024, considers metformin a reasonable first-line alternative and notes that about half may still require insulin. The difference reflects how experts weigh the evidence. Renal function and other contraindications matter.

04 / Medication

Know how to respond
to low glucose.

Shakiness, sweating, hunger, or confusion can be warning signs.

  1. 01

    Below 70 mg/dL

    If awake and able to swallow: take 15 g of fast-acting carbohydrate.

  2. 02

    Wait 15 minutes

    Recheck. Repeat if still below 70 mg/dL.

  3. 03

    After recovery

    Follow your meal/snack plan and tell your team about the low.

If unable to swallow, unconscious, or having a seizure: no food by mouth. Use prescribed glucagon and call emergency services.

Examples and safety
Examples of 15 g carbohydrate include about 4 oz (½ cup) regular juice or glucose tablets according to their label. Severe confusion or inability to self-treat requires another person’s help. Your diabetes team may individualize this plan.

05 / Pregnancy & birth

Your baby’s testing plan
depends on the whole pregnancy.

Ultrasound estimates growth. Other tests assess fetal well-being.

Diet-controlled GDM

Routine extra testing is not automatically needed in every uncomplicated pregnancy.

Medication or other concerns

Fetal testing is often considered around 32 weeks; timing and frequency vary.

Blood pressure, glucose control, growth, and other conditions can change the plan.

What the tests mean
NST (nonstress test) records the fetal heart rate. BPP (biophysical profile) combines ultrasound observations, sometimes with an NST. ACOG suggests considering once- or twice-weekly testing from 32 weeks for medication-controlled GDM, and twice weekly with poor control. Growth scan timing is individualized; estimated weight has uncertainty.

05 / Pregnancy & birth

Well-controlled GDM does not
automatically mean early birth.

Typical delivery windows when there are no other complications:

Diet-controlled · A1GDM 39w0d–40w6d
Medication-controlled · A2GDM 39w0d–39w6d

Poor control or another complication needs a separate, individualized timing decision.

How to interpret these windows
w = weeks; d = days. Diet-controlled GDM alone does not require delivery before 39 weeks; expectant management up to 40w6d may be appropriate with indicated surveillance. These windows are not a promise that every pregnancy can safely wait.

05 / Pregnancy & birth

Glucose checks continue
through birth.

Your care team plans for both you and your baby.

During labor

Your glucose is checked. Insulin or fluids may be adjusted under the hospital’s protocol.

After birth

Your baby’s glucose is checked. Early feeding and additional treatment may be needed.

Ask what to expect if your baby needs extra monitoring.

Hospital care varies
Frequency and maternal intrapartum glucose targets vary by hospital protocol. Newborn screening and treatment depend on symptoms, age after birth, glucose results, and pediatric guidance. Feeding helps but does not guarantee prevention of hypoglycemia.

06 / After birth

Insulin needs usually fall
quickly after delivery.

Once the placenta is delivered, much of pregnancy’s insulin resistance falls.

  1. 01

    Reassess

    Check glucose after delivery.

  2. 02

    Review medication

    GDM-only medication is often stopped under supervision.

  3. 03

    Follow persistent highs

    Ongoing high glucose needs a diabetes evaluation and treatment plan.

Do not assume glucose has returned to normal without follow-up.

06 / After birth

Book your glucose test
for 4–12 weeks after birth.

Before leaving the hospital, ask who will order the test and review the result.

75 g fasting, 2-hour oral glucose tolerance test
at 4–12 weeks postpartum.

A1C alone is less suitable this soon after birth.

Postpartum testing context
Blood loss and pregnancy-related red cell changes can affect A1C. The OGTT uses nonpregnancy criteria postpartum. ACOG also recognizes testing during the delivery admission as an alternative in some settings; coordinate interpretation and follow-up with the care team.

06 / After birth

Follow-up protects
your future health.

A history of GDM raises your future risk of type 2 diabetes.

  1. 01

    Every 1–3 years

    Repeat diabetes screening, even if the postpartum result was normal.

  2. 02

    Between pregnancies

    Keep sustainable food and activity habits; request feeding support if desired.

  3. 03

    Before another pregnancy

    Check for diabetes and plan care before conception.

Breastfeeding has health benefits, but it does not replace diabetes screening.

07 / Take home

Five actions
worth remembering.

You do not have to manage GDM alone.

  1. Complete the right diagnostic test.
  2. Know your fasting and postmeal targets.
  3. Share patterns and ask for help early.
  4. Follow your individualized pregnancy and birth plan.
  5. Keep your postpartum glucose appointment.

A workable plan matters more than blame or perfection.

07 / Evidence

Strong evidence guides care.
Some questions remain.

Guidelines combine research with clinical judgment.

Well supported

Treating diagnosed GDM reduces important growth and birth complications.

Still being studied

Who benefits most from early treatment or CGM in GDM; long-term effects of fetal medication exposure.

Experts differ

How to weigh insulin and metformin as initial medication choices.

Ask how the evidence applies to your own pregnancy.

Research context
TOBOGM found a modest reduction in a composite neonatal outcome with early treatment in an at-risk population, without a material effect on pregnancy-related hypertension. This does not establish one early-pregnancy treatment threshold for everyone. CGM studies differ in populations, devices, and outcomes.

  • When would medication help?

  • What is my birth plan?
