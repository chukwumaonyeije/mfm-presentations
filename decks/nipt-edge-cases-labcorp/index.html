Audience Warm-Up

Audience decision point

A 35-year-old G2P1 at 10w2d asks: “Which NIPT should I get?” Normal early ultrasound. BMI 27. No family history. What is your first counseling move?

  1. Recommend the broadest genome-wide test because more information is always better
  2. Review screening vs diagnostic testing, patient values, fetal sex/SCA preferences, and what she would do with uncertain findings
  3. Avoid cfDNA and order only serum screening
  4. Recommend diagnostic testing for all patients
Teaching point: the “best” test starts with informed preference, not reflexive test expansion.

Guideline Guardrails to Keep in View

  • cfDNA should be available to obstetric patients as a high-performing screen for common aneuploidies.
  • Patients should retain the right to pursue or decline screening and diagnostic testing after counseling.
  • Positive and no-call results require counseling, ultrasound evaluation, and discussion of diagnostic testing.
  • Routine general-population screening for microdeletions or large genome-wide CNVs is not recommended by SMFM; diagnostic testing is preferred when fetal CNV information is the primary goal.

Edge Case 1: No-Call / Low Fetal Fraction

Audience decision point

A patient at 11w0d has a “no-call” due to low fetal fraction. BMI 42. Ultrasound is normal. What do you tell her first?

  1. This is basically negative because nothing abnormal was reported
  2. Repeat testing is mandatory and diagnostic testing is unnecessary
  3. A no-call is not a negative result; it needs counseling, ultrasound review, and a discussion of repeat cfDNA vs diagnostic testing
  4. Proceed directly to pregnancy termination

Low Fetal Fraction: What It Usually Means

Common contributors

  • Early gestational age
  • Higher maternal BMI
  • Sample quality or technical issues
  • Placental dysfunction
  • Some aneuploidies, especially T13/T18/triploidy patterns in some platforms

Clinical interpretation

  • No-call ≠ screen-negative
  • Repeat may be reasonable in selected cases
  • Diagnostic testing should be offered when clinical concern is high or repeat failure occurs
Never let “insufficient data” be heard as “reassuring data.”

Counseling Language: No-Call

“This result does not say the baby is affected, but it also does not say the baby is unaffected. The test did not have enough usable placental DNA signal to give a reliable answer. Our next step is to combine ultrasound findings, your baseline risk, and your preferences to decide between repeating screening and moving to diagnostic testing.”

Edge Case 3: Vanishing Twin

Audience decision point

A patient had early twin gestation after IVF. One sac demised at 8 weeks. At 12 weeks, cfDNA is positive for T21. Current ultrasound shows one normal fetus. What is the key counseling point?

  1. The surviving fetus definitely has T21
  2. The result may reflect residual cfDNA from the demised co-twin; diagnostic testing should be discussed
  3. Ignore the result because vanishing twin makes all cfDNA invalid
  4. Repeat at 13 weeks will always fix the problem

Vanishing Twin: The Signal Problem

Mechanism

Residual cfDNA from a demised co-twin can persist and confound the risk estimate for the ongoing fetus.

Practical response

  • Document early ultrasound history
  • Clarify timing of demise
  • Use ultrasound correlation
  • Offer diagnostic testing if the result would change management
The test may be accurately detecting DNA from the pregnancy—but not necessarily from the surviving fetus.

Edge Case 4: Twins and Higher-Order Multiples

Useful

  • cfDNA is supported as a first-line option for trisomy 21 in twin gestations
  • Data for T18/T13 in twins are increasingly supportive but affected numbers remain smaller

Be cautious

  • Sex chromosome aneuploidy screening in twins is not routinely recommended by SMFM
  • Higher-order multiples remain a difficult evidence space
  • Assigning a signal to one fetus can be challenging
SMFM Consult Series #74

Edge Case 5: Maternal CNV or Mosaicism

Clue

cfDNA signal does not fit fetal ultrasound or diagnostic testing.

Potential sources

  • Benign maternal copy-number variant
  • Maternal sex chromosome mosaicism
  • Transplant history or transfusion context
  • Maternal malignancy, rarely
The “fetal” screen is performed in a maternal bloodstream. Maternal biology is always in the assay.

Genome-Wide Screening: Power and Discipline

Power

  • All chromosome-pair assessment
  • Large CNV gains/losses above threshold
  • Signals that may explain atypical ultrasound findings

Discipline

  • Lower PPV for rarer findings
  • More incidental or maternal findings
  • Need for genetics and diagnostic confirmation
  • Avoid routine broad screening when guidelines discourage it

Rare Autosomal Trisomy Signal

Think biologically

  • True fetal mosaicism
  • Confined placental mosaicism
  • Placental dysfunction risk

Clinical moves

  • Detailed anatomic survey
  • Genetic counseling
  • Discuss amniocentesis
  • Consider growth surveillance when CPM is plausible
For rare autosomal trisomies, the clinical question is often not only “is the fetus affected?” but also “what does this tell us about the placenta?”

Reading the Report: A Structured Review

  1. Result category: negative, positive, no-call, atypical, genome-wide finding.
  2. Test scope: what was actually ordered and reported?
  3. Fetal fraction / quality: reportable? borderline? redraw?
  4. Pregnancy context: GA, BMI, twins, vanishing twin, IVF/donor egg.
  5. Phenotype: ultrasound normal, soft markers, structural anomaly, growth concern.
  6. Decision: repeat screening, diagnostic testing, genetics, maternal evaluation, surveillance.

PPV, Prevalence, and the Counseling Trap

High sensitivity is not the same as high PPV.

PPV changes with condition prevalence, patient risk, test scope, and result type.

Patient-centered framing

“This is a high-risk screen. The chance that it is truly fetal depends on the condition, your clinical context, and ultrasound findings. Diagnostic testing is how we confirm.”

Patient Counseling Script

“NIPT is one of our strongest screening tools, but it is still a screening test. It looks at DNA fragments in your blood, most of which come from the placenta. When the result is positive, complex, or inconclusive, we use ultrasound and—when you want a definitive fetal answer—diagnostic testing.”

Documentation Template

NIPT result reviewed: [negative / positive / no-call / atypical / genome-wide].
Discussed that cfDNA is a screening test and primarily reflects placental DNA in maternal plasma.
Reviewed limitations, PPV/residual risk, ultrasound findings, and differential source of signal.
Options discussed: repeat cfDNA, genetic counseling, diagnostic testing with CVS/amnio, and/or maternal evaluation as indicated.

Interactive Case 1: No-Call

Scenario

  • 38-year-old at 10w4d
  • BMI 41
  • No-call: low fetal fraction
  • NT ultrasound not yet performed
Audience decision point

What is your next best step?

  1. Tell her the test is negative
  2. Repeat immediately only
  3. Ultrasound + counseling + offer repeat vs diagnostic testing depending on findings/preferences
  4. Order serum screen and ignore cfDNA

Case 1 Reveal

Best answer: Ultrasound + counseling + shared decision-making.

Repeat testing may be reasonable if early gestational age or BMI plausibly contributed, but no-call is not reassuring. If ultrasound is abnormal, if baseline risk is high, or if repeat fails, escalate to diagnostic testing.

Interactive Case 2: Positive T18, Normal Ultrasound

Scenario

  • 12w6d singleton
  • NIPT high risk for trisomy 18
  • Early anatomy appears normal
  • Patient wants certainty before making decisions
Audience decision point

How do you counsel?

  1. High-risk screen confirms fetal T18
  2. Repeat NIPT to confirm
  3. Discuss PPV and offer diagnostic testing; amnio is preferred for fetal confirmation when CPM is a concern
  4. No further testing is needed because ultrasound is normal

Case 2 Reveal

Best answer: Genetic counseling + diagnostic testing.

A normal ultrasound lowers suspicion but does not erase the risk. A screen-positive result requires confirmatory diagnostic testing before irreversible decisions.

Interactive Case 3: Vanishing Twin

Scenario

  • IVF pregnancy
  • Two sacs at 6 weeks; one demised by 8 weeks
  • NIPT at 12 weeks high risk for T21
  • Surviving fetus appears normal
Audience decision point

What is the most important interpretive limitation?

  1. cfDNA may represent the demised co-twin rather than the surviving fetus
  2. T21 is excluded by the normal ultrasound
  3. Repeat NIPT always resolves this
  4. Diagnostic testing is contraindicated

Case 3 Reveal

Best answer: Signal-source uncertainty.

Document the early twin history. Discuss that the result may not belong to the ongoing fetus, and offer diagnostic testing if the answer will affect management.

Interactive Case 4: Multiple Aneuploidies

Scenario

  • Singleton pregnancy
  • Genome-wide cfDNA reports multiple chromosomal abnormalities
  • Detailed ultrasound normal
  • Amniocentesis normal
Audience decision point

What should be considered next?

  1. No further action because amnio is normal
  2. Maternal CNV/mosaicism, CPM, and rarely maternal malignancy
  3. Repeat the same test until negative
  4. Terminate based on screening result

Case 4 Reveal

Best answer: Source-of-signal analysis.

Work with genetics and the laboratory. Consider placental and maternal explanations. Any maternal evaluation should be coordinated, documented, and proportionate to the pattern and clinical context.

Key Takeaways

  1. NIPT is powerful screening, not diagnosis.
  2. The signal is usually placental DNA in maternal plasma.
  3. Edge cases are not exceptions to the biology; they reveal the biology.
  4. Labcorp’s counting-based, genome-wide-capable framework can be discussed credibly when paired with limitations and algorithms.
  5. Positive, no-call, atypical, and genome-wide findings require structured counseling and confirmatory pathways.

Closing Thought

The future of NIPT is not simply more data.
It is better clinical interpretation of biologic signal.
