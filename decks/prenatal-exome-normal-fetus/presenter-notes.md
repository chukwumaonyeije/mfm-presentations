# Prenatal Exome Sequencing in Structurally Normal Fetuses: Critical Appraisal

OpenMFM · Presenter notes · 40 slides

Primary reference: Sotiriadis et al. Ultrasound Obstet Gynecol. 2025;65(5):552–559. https://doi.org/10.1002/uog.29195

Scope: appraisal of this 2025 paper; not a systematic update through 2026.

## 1. Prenatal exome sequencing in structurally normal fetuses

Open with the clinical tension: sequencing may reveal variation when ultrasound offers no recognizable phenotype. This presentation critically appraises one 2025 meta-analysis using the supplied assessment. It is an educational review, not a new systematic review or an institutional guideline. Suggested duration: 40–50 minutes, including discussion.

Sources: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12047680/

## 2. The clinical decision comes before the technology

PES means prenatal exome sequencing. CMA means chromosomal microarray analysis. The review allowed normal karyotype or CMA; the actual four cohorts all followed a normal CMA. Do not equate reassuring cfDNA screening with normal diagnostic CMA. Ask learners which outcome would change their recommendation: a molecular result, a confirmed childhood diagnosis, or demonstrated improvement in care.

Sources: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12047680/

## 3. Three questions organize the appraisal

The presentation is designed for MFM clinicians, genetics teams, residents, and fellows. The clinical practice judgments are the supplied appraisal’s interpretation, not recommendations formally issued by the study investigators or an adopted APA policy.

Sources: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12047680/

## 4. A small molecular yield; an unresolved clinical benefit

P/LP means pathogenic or likely pathogenic. Reciprocal frequencies are rounded from the pooled point estimates, not from a screening-benefit calculation. A severe disease-associated variant is not a 0.5% proven risk of severe disease in a future child. Severity, penetrance, expressivity, and age at onset require separate evaluation.

Sources: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12047680/

## 5. Detection, prediction, and utility are separate claims

In a phenotype-negative prenatal setting, a molecular finding may establish a diagnosis for some conditions, indicate predisposition for others, or create substantial prognostic uncertainty. We should not assume these possibilities are interchangeable. Clinical utility may include informed reproductive decisions and care planning, not only measurable neonatal treatment effects.

Sources: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12047680/

## 6. A registered systematic review of observational cohorts

Databases included PubMed, Cochrane Library, Google Scholar, Scopus, International HTA, and ClinicalTrials.gov. Inclusion was restricted to English-language reports. Eligible case series required at least 10 fetuses; overlapping samples were checked. These procedures strengthen transparency but cannot repair limitations of the original cohorts.

Sources: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12047680/; https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pmed.1003583

## 7. Eligibility was broader than the actual evidence

Trio analysis compares fetal and parental sequences and helps identify de novo variants and phase recessive variants. The actual cohorts do not directly establish the same yield after karyotype alone. Clinical selection and the completeness of prenatal assessment differed across studies.

Sources: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12047680/

## 8. A single-arm yield analysis cannot test a universal policy

Incremental here refers to findings beyond prior conventional testing. It is not a risk difference from a trial of sequencing versus no sequencing. Avoid interpreting the number needed to test to find a variant as a number needed to test to prevent disease or improve outcomes.

Sources: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12047680/

## 9. The statistical model fits uncommon events

The investigators used metaprop in R and a random-effects GLMM. Do not treat the pooled 1.6% as the simple arithmetic average of cohort percentages or as an exact population prevalence. A confidence interval describes uncertainty around the pooled estimate; it is not a prediction interval for every new clinical population.

Sources: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12047680/

## 10. One cohort contributes more than half the fetuses

The meta-analysis reports these denominators and positive counts in Table 1. Levy provides 53.2% of fetuses and 65.6% of positive cases. Those proportions are not GLMM study weights, which the authors did not calculate. Cohort differences include parental selection, reporting policies, and ultrasound definitions. Do not infer geography explains the differences causally.

Sources: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12047680/

## 11. Confidence intervals matter as much as point estimates

Any P/LP: 1.6% (1.0–2.6%); severe: 0.5% (0.1–1.5%); moderate: 0.5% (0.2–1.5%); de novo moderate/severe: 0.7% (0.4–1.2%). The 0.7% subgroup overlaps the overall and severity categories. These estimates are separately pooled and should not be reconstructed by subtracting or adding point estimates. The chart is newly drawn from published summary values, not a reproduction of the article’s forest plot.

Sources: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12047680/

## 12. Most positive cases involved dominant conditions

The separately pooled yields were 1.1% for autosomal dominant findings (95% CI 0.6–2.3%) and 0.3% for autosomal recessive findings (0.1–0.6%). There was only one X-linked recessive case, so its yield could not be robustly pooled. Counts among positives and proportions among all tested fetuses have different denominators.

Sources: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12047680/

## 13. De novo findings are the most provocative signal

Eighteen of 32 positive cases were de novo; eight autosomal dominant variants were inherited, six maternally and two paternally. The 14 moderate/severe de novo cases are a subset, not additional cases. Trio sequencing can recognize these events, but confirming de novo status does not by itself resolve penetrance or clinical severity in this fetus.

Sources: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12047680/

## 14. Severity describes an associated condition, not a certain outcome

These counts sum to 32. Severity classification was a post-hoc subanalysis by clinical experts, with reclassification when needed. A universal prenatal disease-severity standard is lacking. Classification concerns the expected associated disease, not a confirmed individual phenotype. The single profound case should not silently be merged into the published severe estimate.

Sources: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12047680/; https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0114391

## 15. Several design choices strengthen the review

Avoid dismissing the paper because it contains only four studies. It provides a baseline for a practice already occurring. Its main strengths are transparency, appropriate synthesis, and clear discussion of interpretive limits. At the same time, some severity and inheritance analyses were post hoc and deserve cautious interpretation.

Sources: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12047680/

## 16. The classification of a “positive” result may change

VUS means variant of uncertain significance; the article also uses VOUS. This was a published independent critique, not a definitive reclassification of every included fetus. The original review notes differences in application of ACMG/ClinGen criteria. A P/LP label evaluates disease causation in a defined context; it is not a guarantee of disease, prognosis, or actionability. The investigators proposed reinterpretation after 24–36 months.

Sources: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12047680/; https://doi.org/10.1002/pd.6687; https://doi.org/10.1038/gim.2015.30

## 17. Longitudinal phenotype confirmation is missing

No systematic postnatal confirmation does not mean every molecular result was incorrect or every child lacked follow-up of any kind. It means sufficient data were not reported to perform the review’s planned analyses. Developmental, neurobehavioral, and adult-onset conditions may require years of follow-up.

Sources: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12047680/

## 18. These were selected families in specific health systems

The population is not equivalent to consecutive unselected patients at routine anatomy screening. A known prior familial disorder was tested before inclusion where relevant, but prior history and parental concerns may still shape selection. Country itself is not the causal factor; screening programs, access, consent, referral patterns, and family selection all matter.

Sources: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12047680/

## 19. “Structurally normal” does not necessarily mean low risk

The review’s discussion identifies incomplete exclusion of FGR as a limitation. Table 1 explicitly lists IUGR among Levy’s exclusions, so saying that no study excluded FGR would be inaccurate. The evidence does not isolate the contribution of growth abnormalities, and it does not provide an isolated-FGR subgroup estimate.

Sources: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12047680/

## 20. The burden of uncertain and secondary findings is unclear

Gao included adult-onset secondary findings among positive fetal cases, whereas Daum excluded a RET secondary finding from primary diagnoses. The authors note that exchanging selected classifications would leave the headline yield at 1.6%, while changing what that yield means clinically. Discuss consent, future child autonomy, incidental parental findings, and reporting preferences without implying a known rate of harm.

Sources: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12047680/

## 21. Low risk of bias does not establish high certainty

Because these were single-arm cohorts, the comparability section was removed from NOS, reducing the maximum to six stars. The resulting scores are reasonable within that framework. They should not be conflated with high certainty of an individual fetus’s prognosis or a proven clinical benefit of a sequencing program.

Sources: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12047680/

## 22. Clinical utility remains unproven

Absence of evidence of benefit is not proof of no benefit. Some condition-specific results may be highly useful. The missing claim is that a routine policy creates net benefit across otherwise reassuring pregnancies. This would require management, outcome, patient-reported, and economic evidence beyond variant counts.

Sources: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12047680/

## 23. What might testing 10,000 fetuses reveal?

Each number is the published pooled proportion multiplied by 10,000. Scaled CIs describe uncertainty in the expected count from the pooled estimate, not prediction intervals for an actual future cohort. The estimates are not additive. There is no single observed cohort of 10,000 in this paper. The uncertainty range for severe-associated findings is especially wide.

Sources: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12047680/

## 24. A 0.5% severe-associated yield supports two reasonable views

The 99.5% complement applies only to the severe-associated finding category. It does not mean 99.5% have no genetic condition, no other finding, or guaranteed normal outcomes. Some families value information highly despite uncertain immediate actionability; others prefer to avoid ambiguous prognostic information. Good counseling makes those preferences explicit.

Sources: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12047680/

## 25. Three clinical situations require different testing approaches

These are the supplied critical appraisal’s practice judgments. They are not trial-derived treatment recommendations. Targeted familial testing may be appropriate even with entirely normal anatomy. For structural anomalies, sequence interpretation is strengthened by a defined phenotype. ISPD guidance supports responsible clinical use in selected pregnancies and does not endorse routine sequencing for all pregnancies.

Sources: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12047680/; https://doi.org/10.1002/pd.6157

## 26. Anomaly-focused sequencing has a different evidence base

These comparator figures are context cited in the reviewed paper, not new pooled estimates produced by this review. They should not be used as a universal yield for every anomalous fetus or as a head-to-head comparison with the normal-anatomy cohorts. The two populations differ in prior probability and interpretability.

Sources: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12047680/

## 27. A request for expanded testing deserves a structured discussion

Ask what a result would change, including pregnancy decisions, neonatal planning, or future reproduction. Clarify the laboratory’s reporting policy and options for reinterpretation. Explain that a negative exome does not exclude all genetic disease. Sequencing methods and coverage can miss some variant classes; CMA and exome answer different questions.

Sources: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12047680/; https://doi.org/10.1002/pd.6157

## 28. Explain the yield without promising a diagnosis

Use plain language. Pathogenic and likely pathogenic are classifications of a variant’s relationship to disease, not individual outcome guarantees. Some variants have variable penetrance or expression. For a given condition, the evidence may support more or less certainty than this general discussion. Offer written materials and time for genetics counseling.

Sources: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12047680/

## 29. Make the uncertainty and the next step explicit

Explain that the severe-associated estimate is imprecise (95% CI 0.1–1.5%) and comes from retrospective selected cohorts. The language is a starting point, not a substitute for individualized informed consent. Ask the patient to explain back the difference between a variant and a predictable outcome, and explore preferences for uncertain or secondary findings.

Sources: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12047680/

## 30. Growth restriction can be a meaningful genetic phenotype

FGR means fetal growth restriction. The review neither proves that FGR caused the reported molecular yield nor establishes a yield for isolated FGR. The correct response is to examine the phenotype carefully, including growth trajectory, Doppler assessment, structural imaging, gestational age, and family history. Do not transfer a 1.6% general pooled estimate into an FGR-specific counseling statistic.

Sources: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12047680/

## 31. Keep early severe FGR and late isolated FGR distinct

Use established FGR evaluation and surveillance pathways. Genetic assessment may include diagnostic chromosome testing and, in selected circumstances, molecular testing after specialist consultation. This presentation intentionally does not invent an FGR sequencing threshold from a study that did not analyze the relevant subgroup.

Sources: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12047680/

## 32. The next study should confirm disease and usefulness

A larger denominator alone will not solve uncertain variant validity and missing follow-up. A useful prospective registry or comparative study should record the pathway from pretest indication through result disclosure, management decisions, birth, and childhood. Systematically document nonparticipation and attrition to assess selection and follow-up bias.

Sources: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12047680/

## 33. Measure outcomes beyond the number of variants

Comparative designs can test whether sequencing alters care or outcomes relative to usual pathways; universal randomization may not be the only feasible design. Record whether a change in management was beneficial, neutral, or harmful, not merely whether a change occurred. Patient-reported outcomes should respect differing values rather than presuming all additional information is either good or bad.

Sources: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12047680/

## 34. Promising, not practice-changing

These qualitative domain ratings are the supplied appraisal’s interpretation and not a formal GRADE profile. Diagnostic yield is subject to uncertain variant classification and selected recruitment. Clinical benefit is not disproven; it is insufficiently evaluated. Avoid calling this a practice-changing trial or a screening guideline.

Sources: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12047680/

## 35. Use the evidence for education before changing policy

APA refers to the organization named in the supplied brief, consistent with Atlanta Perinatal Associates in existing repository branding. These proposed recommendations should not be displayed as an official policy already approved by APA. Decisions about offering and funding testing require local clinical governance and up-to-date guidance.

Sources: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12047680/

## 36. A result arrives; what has been diagnosed?

No real patient is represented. Do not ask learners to resolve an unnamed variant without the gene, variant evidence, inheritance details, and phenotype. The teaching goal is recognizing what information is missing. Condition-specific consultation may support a firm diagnosis, a probabilistic risk estimate, or a need for reinterpretation and observation.

Sources: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12047680/

## 37. Three checks for interpreting the paper

Use this as a brief knowledge check or ask the audience to answer before revealing the slide. The denominators, categories, and distinction between findings and disease are the highest-value interpretation skills in this review. Each answer should connect to a specific limitation in the methods or source evidence.

Sources: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12047680/

## 38. Counsel with the estimate; preserve the uncertainty

Return to the opening question. This paper advances the evidence for prenatal genomic detection but does not establish the case for universal exome sequencing in structurally normal pregnancies. The 0.7% moderate/severe de novo signal deserves further study, especially because carrier screening does not reliably anticipate those events. The appropriate response is better counseling and better outcome evidence.

Sources: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12047680/
