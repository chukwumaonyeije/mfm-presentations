Prenatal exome sequencing in structurally normal fetuses

What does a molecular finding tell us when fetal anatomy is reassuring?

MATERNAL–FETAL MEDICINE · GENOMICS · EVIDENCE REVIEW

Presenter notes · Opening

Open with the clinical tension: sequencing may reveal variation when ultrasound offers no recognizable phenotype. This presentation critically appraises one 2025 meta-analysis using the supplied assessment. It is an educational review, not a new systematic review or an institutional guideline. Suggested duration: 40–50 minutes, including discussion.

Conceptual framework

Detection, prediction, and utility are separate claims

The study directly estimates the first step.

  1. 01Diagnostic yield

    How often was an additional P/LP variant reported?

  2. 02Predictive validity

    Will this fetus develop clinically important disease?

  3. 03Clinical utility

    Does knowing improve decisions, management, or outcomes?

Evidence for variant detection cannot substitute for evidence at the later steps.

Presenter notes · Conceptual framework

In a phenotype-negative prenatal setting, a molecular finding may establish a diagnosis for some conditions, indicate predisposition for others, or create substantial prognostic uncertainty. We should not assume these possibilities are interchangeable. Clinical utility may include informed reproductive decisions and care planning, not only measurable neonatal treatment effects.

Methods

A registered systematic review of observational cohorts

PROSPERO CRD42024593349 · PRISMA reporting framework.

Search

2018 to September 25, 2024; search repeated before submission. Multiple databases, registers, and reference lists.

Selection

1,030 records → seven full-text articles → four included cohorts. Independent screening and data extraction.

The evidence reflects the search period; publication in 2025 does not make it a current living review.

Presenter notes · Methods

Databases included PubMed, Cochrane Library, Google Scholar, Scopus, International HTA, and ClinicalTrials.gov. Inclusion was restricted to English-language reports. Eligible case series required at least 10 fetuses; overlapping samples were checked. These procedures strengthen transparency but cannot repair limitations of the original cohorts.

OpenMFM · 06 / 40 · Critical appraisal

Methods

Eligibility was broader than the actual evidence

All four included studies were retrospective and used exome sequencing after normal CMA.

Eligible in protocol

Apparently normal fetal phenotype; normal karyotype or CMA; prospective or retrospective cohorts; singleton or twin pregnancies.

Included in practice

Four retrospective cohorts. Mostly trio sequencing; Daum used trio analysis in approximately half of cases.

“Apparently normal” was a prenatal observation, with no systematic confirmation through birth.

Presenter notes · Methods

Trio analysis compares fetal and parental sequences and helps identify de novo variants and phase recessive variants. The actual cohorts do not directly establish the same yield after karyotype alone. Clinical selection and the completeness of prenatal assessment differed across studies.

OpenMFM · 08 / 40 · Critical appraisal

Methods

The statistical model fits uncommon events

Random-effects generalized linear mixed model for pooled proportions.

Why GLMM?

Models binomial outcomes directly and handles sparse events without a continuity correction for zero-event studies.

What remains uncertain?

Only four studies inform the between-study distribution. A pooled mean is not a universal patient-specific probability.

The model pools reported yield; it cannot correct inconsistent classification or selection.

Presenter notes · Methods

The investigators used metaprop in R and a random-effects GLMM. Do not treat the pooled 1.6% as the simple arithmetic average of cohort percentages or as an exact population prevalence. A confidence interval describes uncertainty around the pooled estimate; it is not a prediction interval for every new clinical population.

Critical appraisal · 1

The classification of a “positive” result may change

Independent reanalysis questioned more than half of P/LP variants in one included cohort.

The concern

The authors cite a critique suggesting that over half of the Levy cohort’s P/LP variants could be classified as VUS.

The implication

The 1.6% estimate may overstate robust molecular diagnoses or actionable findings. The magnitude is unresolved.

Do not halve the pooled yield: no validated reclassification of the full dataset was performed.

Presenter notes · Critical appraisal · 1

VUS means variant of uncertain significance; the article also uses VOUS. This was a published independent critique, not a definitive reclassification of every included fetus. The original review notes differences in application of ACMG/ClinGen criteria. A P/LP label evaluates disease causation in a defined context; it is not a guarantee of disease, prognosis, or actionability. The investigators proposed reinterpretation after 24–36 months.

Low risk of bias does not establish high certainty

The authors used a modified Newcastle–Ottawa Scale for single-arm cohorts.

What the tool showed

Three studies scored 6/6 stars; one scored 5/6. All were labeled low risk of bias.

What the tool cannot settle

Transportability, variant validity, missing longitudinal outcomes, or the net value of universal testing.

Certainty labels in this slideshow are appraisal judgments, not a formal GRADE assessment.

Presenter notes · Critical appraisal

Because these were single-arm cohorts, the comparability section was removed from NOS, reducing the maximum to six stars. The resulting scores are reasonable within that framework. They should not be conflated with high certainty of an individual fetus’s prognosis or a proven clinical benefit of a sequencing program.

A 0.5% severe-associated yield supports two reasonable views

The same estimate can be valued differently by different families.

Case for expanded access

A potentially important diagnosis before birth may enable earlier assessment, specialty planning, or reproductive choices.

Case against routine testing

About 99.5% would have no severe-associated finding, while uncertainty, costs, and outcome benefit remain unresolved.

Presenter notes · Decision analysis

The 99.5% complement applies only to the severe-associated finding category. It does not mean 99.5% have no genetic condition, no other finding, or guaranteed normal outcomes. Some families value information highly despite uncertain immediate actionability; others prefer to avoid ambiguous prognostic information. Good counseling makes those preferences explicit.

Informed choice can coexist with a recommendation against routine use.

Clarify the question

Presenter notes · Practice implications

Ask what a result would change, including pregnancy decisions, neonatal planning, or future reproduction. Clarify the laboratory’s reporting policy and options for reinterpretation. Counseling

Explain the yield without promising a diagnosis

Suggested patient-facing language, adapted from the supplied appraisal.

“When the baby’s anatomy appears normal and chromosome testing is reassuring, exome sequencing may find a pathogenic or likely pathogenic genetic change in about 1–2% of cases. A finding does not always tell us whether a child will develop a significant medical condition.”

Separate the chance of a finding from the chance of an affected child.

Presenter notes · Counseling

Use plain language. Pathogenic and likely pathogenic are classifications of a variant’s relationship to disease, not individual outcome guarantees. Some variants have variable penetrance or expression. For a given condition, the evidence may support more or less certainty than this general discussion. Offer written materials and time for genetics counseling.

Presenter notes · Fetal growth restriction

FGR means fetal growth restriction. The review neither proves that FGR caused the reported molecular yield nor establishes a yield for isolated FGR. Research agenda

The next study should confirm disease and usefulness

A prospective multicenter design should connect variant detection with lived outcomes.

  1. 01Standardize entry

    Imaging, growth, family history, prior testing, and consecutive recruitment.

  2. 02Standardize interpretation

    Trio sequencing, consistent classification, reporting rules, and reinterpretation.

  3. 03Follow through childhood

    Confirm phenotype, penetrance, clinical consequences, and delayed onset.

Presenter notes · Research agenda

A larger denominator alone will not solve uncertain variant validity and missing follow-up. A useful prospective registry or comparative study should record the pathway from pretest indication through result disclosure, management decisions, birth, and childhood. Systematically document nonparticipation and attrition to assess selection and follow-up bias.

Research agenda

Measure outcomes beyond the number of variants

A sequencing program must be evaluated as a care pathway.

Clinical and prognostic outcomes

  • Confirmed clinically significant diagnoses
  • Changes in prenatal and neonatal management
  • Penetrance, severity, and age at onset

Patient and system outcomes

  • Uncertain, incidental, and secondary findings
  • Psychological effects and decisional regret
  • Costs, access, and cost-effectiveness

Follow-up must extend beyond the newborn period for delayed-onset conditions.

Presenter notes · Research agenda

Comparative designs can test whether sequencing alters care or outcomes relative to usual pathways; universal randomization may not be the only feasible design. Record whether a change in management was beneficial, neutral, or harmful, not merely whether a change occurred. Patient-reported outcomes should respect differing values rather than presuming all additional information is either good or bad.

Universal PES is not established.

Use genetics expertise, condition-specific interpretation, and shared decision-making.

Presenter notes · Take-home message

Return to the opening question. This paper advances the evidence for prenatal genomic detection but does not establish the case for universal exome sequencing in structurally normal pregnancies. The 0.7% moderate/severe de novo signal deserves further study, especially because carrier screening does not reliably anticipate those events. The appropriate response is better counseling and better outcome evidence.
